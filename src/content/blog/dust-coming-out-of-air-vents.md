You dust the living room on Sunday and by Tuesday the coffee table is gray again. Or you see a faint puff leave a ceiling register every time the air kicks on. Either way, your system is moving dust through the house, and the instinct is to book a duct cleaning.

Sometimes that is the answer. More often, the dust is getting into the system somewhere it should not, and a cleaning on its own will not stop it.

## Start with where the air comes in

Your air handler pulls air from the house through the return, runs it through the filter, cools it and pushes it out the supply registers. The filter is only useful if every bit of air passes through it. **Any opening on the return side, between the house and the blower, lets unfiltered air in.** That air carries attic dust, garage grime, insulation fibers and pollen straight to your registers.

That is why the two most common causes of dusty vents have nothing to do with dirty ducts.

### 1. The filter does not fit

A filter that is a size too small, bowed, installed backwards or sitting in a grille that does not close leaves a gap, and air takes the easy route. Pull the filter and look at the frame. A dark line along one edge shows where air is going around it. Fix the fit before you change anything else.

### 2. The return is leaking

This is what we find most. In Florida the air handler usually sits in a hall closet or the garage on a return platform built on site, and over the years those seams open up. The blower then draws air from the wall cavity, the garage or the attic. In many Naples houses, sealing a leaky return platform in the garage is the single most useful fix, as the [Naples service area page](/service-areas/naples/) explains. The tell is dark dust streaks on the insulation or framing around the air handler base, marking each spot where air is being pulled in.

### 3. The ducts really are dirty

If the system has never been cleaned, or someone sanded drywall with the air running, there is a layer stuck to the branches, the plenum and the blower wheel, and every startup sheds a little. New construction does this too. Fine white or gray dust in a house less than a couple of years old is often construction debris.

### 4. A torn or disconnected duct

A flex run that has pulled off its collar in the attic, or one that has been crushed, pulls in attic air and insulation. Yellow or pink fibers on the register are the giveaway.

## What the color tells you

- **Fine gray:** household dust and lint, from dirty ducts or a return pulling from the closet.
- **White, chalky:** drywall or construction dust.
- **Yellow or pink fibers:** insulation from a damaged duct or a deteriorating duct board liner.
- **Yellow green in spring:** pollen getting past the filter, nearly always through the return.
- **Black specks that return:** possibly mold. Read our guide to [black mold on air vents](/blog/black-mold-air-vents/) before you scrub it with anything.

## The paper towel test

1. Fix the filter fit so nothing can go around it.
2. Tape a white paper towel loosely over one supply register and run the system for a day.
3. If the towel comes back gray with a good filter in place, the dust is coming from inside the system or from a leak downstream of the filter.

Do not open the air handler panels to look. There is live voltage inside even with the thermostat off.

## If the dust started after a cleaning

A proper cleaning connects a negative air machine to the plenum, seals every register and works each branch while the system is under suction. The blower wheel and the coil face get cleaned too. If a crew ran a vacuum at the register openings, they moved dust around rather than removing it. If they cleaned only the supply side, the return keeps feeding dirt back in. And if the real cause was a return leak, no amount of cleaning closes it. Our article on [what to expect from an air duct cleaning](/blog/what-to-expect-air-duct-cleaning/) lays out what a complete job looks like.

## What fixes it

- **Filter fit:** you, for a few dollars.
- **Return leaks and loose ducts:** [air duct repair](/services/air-duct-repair/). A pressure test measures how much air the system loses, the price follows from that, from $250 to $2,000 and up depending on scope, and the test runs again when the work is done.
- **Built up dirt:** [air duct cleaning](/services/air-duct-cleaning/), every supply and return under negative air plus the blower wheel and coil face, $300 to $600 per system.
