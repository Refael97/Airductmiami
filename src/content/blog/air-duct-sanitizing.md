Sanitizing is one of the most consistently oversold items in this trade. It gets added to quotes by default, described in language that makes declining it sound reckless, and applied to systems where it does nothing that the cleaning had not already done.

It is also, in a specific set of circumstances, genuinely useful. Those circumstances are narrower than the way it is sold.

## The short answer

Air duct sanitizing is the application of an EPA-registered antimicrobial to ductwork after it has been mechanically cleaned. It costs $75 to $200 as an add-on in Florida.

It is worth doing after confirmed mold, for an odor that survived the cleaning, in heavy pet households, or where someone in the home is immune compromised. On a system that was simply dusty, mechanical cleaning is the job and sanitizing adds little.

One thing to establish first: the EPA registers these products **only** for bare sheet metal duct, and **no** product is registered for the fiberglass-lined duct that most Florida homes actually have.

It is never a substitute for cleaning. A biocide sprayed over a layer of accumulated debris does not reach the surfaces underneath it, which is where anything living actually is.

## What it is, mechanically

After the ducts have been cleaned under negative pressure, an antimicrobial is introduced into the system, usually as a fog, so it coats the interior surfaces. The product is left to dwell for the period its label specifies, and the system is aired before it goes back into normal use.

The two things that determine whether this does anything are the order and the surface. Applied after proper source removal, onto a clean hard surface, it does what it says. Applied before or instead of cleaning, or onto porous material, it mostly does not.

## What the EPA actually says

This matters more than the marketing, and it is easy to check.

The EPA's guidance, [Should You Have the Air Ducts in Your Home Cleaned?](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/should-you-have-air-ducts-your-home-cleaned), is narrower than most people expect. The products used for this are **registered solely for sanitizing the smooth surfaces of unlined, bare sheet metal ducts**. **No product is registered as a biocide for use on fiberglass duct board or fiberglass-lined ducts.**

That second sentence covers a large share of Florida ductwork, because flexible duct with an internal fiberglass liner is the standard build here.

The EPA is also direct about what happens once that liner is contaminated: cleaning is not sufficient to prevent regrowth, and there is no registered biocide for treating porous duct material. In other words, for the most common duct type in this state, the honest options after mold are replacement of the affected sections or living with recurrence. They are not a treatment.

The practical consequences:

- Establish what material your ducts are before agreeing to any biocide. This is a two minute question, not an inspection.
- Ask which product is being used and ask to see the label. A technician doing this properly carries it and will not mind.
- If your ducts are fiberglass lined and someone is offering to sanitize them, they are applying a product outside its registered use. That is worth knowing before rather than after.

## When it earns its place

Four situations where we recommend it without hesitation:

- **After confirmed mold remediation.** The mechanical work removes the growth; the antimicrobial addresses what remains on the cleaned surfaces. Here it is part of the job rather than an add-on.
- **An odor that survived cleaning.** If the musty smell is gone from the coil and still present in the airstream, there is biological material somewhere the brush did not reach.
- **Heavy pet households.** Dander and organic material feed growth, and the load rebuilds faster than in a pet-free home.
- **An immune-compromised occupant.** Someone undergoing chemotherapy, a transplant recipient, or a person with a diagnosed mold sensitivity. Here the calculation is different and the margin is worth buying.

## When it does not

- **A normally dirty system.** Dust from ordinary living is not a microbial problem. Removing it is the whole job.
- **On fiberglass-lined duct.** There is no EPA-registered product for it, whatever is being offered.
- **Instead of cleaning, at a lower price.** This is the version to walk away from. "Sanitizing treatment" offered as a cheaper alternative to cleaning is a fog applied over a layer of debris.
- **On a schedule.** Sanitizing every three years alongside every cleaning is not a maintenance practice, it is a line item. There should be a reason each time.
- **As "duct fogging" with no product named.** If nobody will tell you what is being sprayed into the air your household breathes, that is the answer.

## The Florida angle, and its limit

Florida genuinely does have more of the conditions that make sanitizing relevant than most of the country. Humidity above 60 percent for much of the year keeps the evaporator coil and the duct interior damp, and damp surfaces with a dust supply are where growth establishes.

But that argument has a limit that gets left out when it is used to sell. **The humidity is still there after the treatment.** An antimicrobial does not change the dew point in your house. If indoor humidity stays above 60 percent, growth returns, and the money would have been better spent on the [humidity problem](/blog/ideal-indoor-humidity-florida/) than on the symptom.

So the honest sequence is: clean the system, get the humidity under 60 percent, and sanitize where one of the four reasons above applies. In that order.
