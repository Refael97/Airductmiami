Something white is drifting out from behind your dryer, or the laundry room fills with a haze every time you run a load. Before anything else, you need to know which of two very different things you are looking at. One is a fire. The other is a vent problem that is slowly soaking your laundry room and, on a gas dryer, may be doing something worse.

## First: smoke means get out

If it is gray, brown or dark, smells like burning, plastic or rubber, or seems to come from inside the dryer or its control panel, treat it as smoke.

- **Keep the dryer door closed.** Opening it feeds air to lint that may be smoldering.
- **Get everyone out of the house**, pets too.
- **Call 911 from outside.** Do not go back in to check on it.

If you cannot tell which it is, treat it as smoke. Being wrong in that direction costs you nothing.

## Telling steam from smoke

Steam is white, has no smell (or smells like clean laundry), clears in seconds and leaves beads of water on the walls, the ceiling or the window. Smoke hangs in the air, smells burnt and leaves nothing wet behind. A burning smell with no visible smoke is not a reason to relax: it is the step before smoke, and the same causes are behind it.

## Why steam ends up indoors

A dryer only works in one direction. It heats air, pushes it through the wet load to pick up moisture, and sends it out of the house through the vent. When something in that path blocks the air, it leaks out at the easiest point, which is usually a loose joint behind the machine or the door seal.

The causes we find most often, in order:

1. **Lint packed into the duct.** The lint screen does not catch everything. What gets past it sticks to the duct wall, mostly at elbows and on long runs. In Florida many dryers vent up through the roof, so the run is long, and the humid air in a laundry room makes the lint tacky enough to build up fast.
2. **A transition hose that has been crushed or pulled off.** The dryer gets pushed back against the wall after every mop and move. A foil hose folds flat, or slips off the dryer's outlet collar, and every load after that vents straight into the room.
3. **A blocked hood outside.** A bird screen, a flap glued shut with lint, or a nest. If the flap does not open while the dryer runs, the air has nowhere to go. Our guide to [a dryer vent flap that won't open](/blog/dryer-vent-flap-not-opening/) covers each one.
4. **A separated joint inside the wall or attic.** The exhaust escapes before it reaches the hood and soaks the space it escapes into.

Steam staying indoors matters more in Florida than in most places. Every load puts a lot of water into the air, and if that water stays in a closed laundry room or an attic it turns into peeling paint, damp drywall and a musty smell. The restriction also traps heat, which is how a steaming dryer becomes [a dryer that overheats and shuts off](/blog/dryer-overheating-shutting-off/) a few weeks later.

## If your dryer runs on gas

A gas dryer vents more than moisture and lint. The burner's exhaust, including **carbon monoxide**, leaves the house through the same duct. Carbon monoxide has no color and no smell, so the steam you can see is the only visible sign that the exhaust you cannot see is staying inside too.

- Steam indoors from a gas dryer: turn it off, close the gas valve behind it, and do not run it until the vent is connected and clear.
- Headaches, dizziness or nausea that ease when people leave the house, or a carbon monoxide alarm going off: get out and call 911.
- A rotten egg or sulfur smell is gas, not steam. Do not flip switches. Leave and call the gas company or 911 from outside.

## The five minute check

With a clean lint screen, run the dryer on heat with a couple of damp towels. After about five minutes, go to the outside hood. You want a visible plume, the flap fully open and a strong stream of warm air. Weak air means the restriction is between the dryer and the outside wall. Then, with the dryer off and cool, pull it out a few inches and look at the hose. Crushed or disconnected is your answer. If the vent exits through the roof, stay off the roof; the hose and the dryer's behavior tell you enough. If you are still unsure, the [signs of a clogged dryer vent](/blog/signs-of-clogged-dryer-vent/) are a good second check.
