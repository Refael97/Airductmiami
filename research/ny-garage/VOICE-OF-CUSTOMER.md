# Voice of customer: garage door services, New York

Deep dive on pass 9c. Sources: public review text on Google, Yelp,
HomeAdvisor, Angi, Trustindex and company review pages; competitor FAQ and
homepage copy; consumer-protection and scam-warning content.

The point of this file is language. What customers actually say, in their
words, becomes our headlines, our FAQ questions, and the agent's script.

---

## 1. The single most valuable finding

**Everyone in this industry already claims transparency. Almost nobody
publishes a number.**

Real competitor homepage lines, collected verbatim:

> "UPFRONT PRICING. Our techs are non-commissioned. No hidden fees, ever."
> "No surprises here. Clear, upfront pricing with detailed estimates."
> "We provide upfront pricing before starting any work, no hidden fees or
> surprise charges."
> "We will never charge any hidden fees, trip charges or use gimmicks."

Every one of those pages then says **"call for a quote"**. The claim is
table stakes; the proof is missing. That is why Angi, HomeGuide and This Old
House own every cost query in this vertical.

**Our differentiation is not saying "upfront pricing". It is printing the
numbers on the page**, per job, for New York, and standing behind them. The
line to own is closer to:

> "Here is what it costs. Before you call."

## 2. What the scam actually looks like (and the numbers that expose it)

Documented pattern, in escalating order:

1. **Advertised bait**: a spring replacement at $49 to $89, or a "$29
   service charge". One New York competitor leads with exactly that.
2. **On arrival**: the technician finds cables, drums, bearings and rollers
   that "must" be replaced, tripling or quadrupling the bill.
3. **The tiered spring pitch**: 90-day spring vs 2-year vs "lifetime",
   described in consumer forums as "the oldest scam in the garage door
   repair industry".
4. **The written-off door**: "this door is basically done", pivoting a
   repair call into a full replacement sale.
5. **Documented outcome**: a customer quoted **$1,500 to replace two
   springs**, who got a second opinion and was told the springs were fine
   and needed a free tension adjustment.

**The exposing fact**: two springs replaced in New York is roughly **$320 to
$700 all in**. A $1,500 quote is a 2 to 4x markup on a job the customer
cannot inspect. Publishing that range is the whole argument.

**Important nuance we must get right**: recommending both springs at once is
**legitimate**, not a scam. When one snaps, the other has identical fatigue
and usually follows within weeks. If we accuse honest upsells of being
scams, we lose credibility with the informed reader. The article has to
teach the difference:

| Legitimate | Bait |
|---|---|
| Replacing both springs when one snapped | Replacing parts that were not inspected in front of you |
| High-cycle springs for a heavy-use or above-garage-bedroom door | High-cycle springs sold to a one-car-a-day household |
| A quote that goes up after seeing bent tracks, explained and shown | A quote that goes up after the door is already apart |
| A written estimate before work | A price named only after the job is done |

## 3. Positive review language (what "good" sounds like in this vertical)

Recurring, near-verbatim:

- "Came out the same day"
- "Arrived on time"
- "Done within 30 minutes"
- "He showed me the broken spring"
- "Explained everything"
- "Gave me options, no pressure"
- "The price was what he quoted"
- "Fair price"
- "Cleaned up after"
- "Honest and courteous"

**Pattern**: not one of these is about craftsmanship. Every single one is
about **speed, visibility, and the quote holding**. In this vertical the
product is trust under stress. That belongs in the hero, the trust block,
the FAQ, and the agent's opening.

## 4. Negative review language (our promises, inverted)

- "Quoted me X, then said it would be Y"
- "Tried to sell me a new door"
- "Charged me for parts I did not need"
- "Told me it was basically written off"
- "The $29 turned into $400"
- "Would not give a price on the phone"
- "Never showed up in the window"

Each one converts directly into a promise we can actually keep:

| Complaint | Our promise |
|---|---|
| Price changed after arrival | The quote you get is the price you pay, in writing before work starts |
| Unnecessary parts | We show you the failed part and explain why, before replacing it |
| Pushed to replace the door | Repair first. We tell you when replacement is genuinely cheaper |
| No price on the phone | Ranges published on the site, honest estimate on the call |
| Missed window | A real arrival window, and a call if anything changes |

## 5. Questions customers actually ask

Ranked by how often they surface in reviews, FAQs and Q&A sections. Each one
becomes an FAQ entry, an article, and a line in the agent's script.

**Before booking**
1. How much does it cost to fix a garage door spring?
2. How soon can you get here? Do you do same day?
3. Do you charge for the estimate or a service call fee?
4. Can I still use my garage door right now?
5. Do I need one spring or two?
6. Is it worth repairing, or should I replace the whole door?
7. Do you service my area?
8. Are you licensed and insured?
9. Do you work nights and weekends?
10. Do you work on my brand of opener?

**During and after**
11. How long does the repair take?
12. What warranty do you give on parts and labor?
13. How long do springs last?
14. Why did it break?
15. What can I do so it does not happen again?

**Answers we can give honestly today** (real market data):
- Repair time: springs or cables 30 min to 2 h; opener install 2 to 4 h;
  most jobs 1 to 3 h
- Warranty norm in the industry: 90 days labor, 1 year parts. We match or
  beat it, and we publish it
- Spring lifespan: about 10,000 cycles, roughly 7 to 10 years at normal use;
  standard 5,000, oil-tempered 10,000, high-cycle far more
- Using the door with a broken spring: **no**. Manual with great care at
  most, never with the opener, which bends panels and burns out the motor

## 6. Positioning problem we must solve

Every established local competitor leads with age:

> "Since 1983" · "Since 1987" · "Since 1988" · "Since 1998" · "Since 1999" ·
> "70+ years" · "family owned"

**A new brand cannot compete on that axis, and we will not fake it.** So the
trust block has to be built from things a new company can genuinely own:

1. **Published prices**, which none of them do
2. **Written estimate before work**, stated as a policy
3. **Licensed and insured**
4. **A real arrival window**, and answering in the customer's language
5. **Named technicians and real job photos**, from the first job onward
6. **A warranty in writing**, published, not "ask us"

Age says "we have been here". Transparency says "you can check us before you
call". The second is the only one available to us, and in a vertical this
full of bait pricing, it may be the stronger one.

## 8. What this changes in the build

1. **A live price table on the homepage**, not buried in a cost article.
   This is the differentiator and it should be visible without scrolling far.
2. **A cost cluster of articles** targeting the queries the directories
   currently own, each with real New York numbers.
3. **A scam-education article** that carefully separates legitimate upsells
   from bait, mirroring what worked on site one.
4. **Emergency framing everywhere**: "car trapped" is the moment of highest
   intent.
5. **The agent's script** opens by confirming the symptom, gives the honest
   published range, promises a written estimate, and books a real window.
   It never names a final price sight unseen.
6. **The FAQ block on every page** is drawn from section 5 in that order.
