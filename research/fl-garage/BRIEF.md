## 2. Voice: how every page reads

Calm, direct, specific. The reader's car is stuck, or their door has a
dent, or a storm is three days out. We answer. We do not sell.

Rules that are checked mechanically before anything ships:

1. **No em dashes, no en dashes.** Not in prose, not in titles, not in
   FAQ answers, not in alt text. Use a comma, a period, or a colon.
2. **No exclamation marks.**
3. **No "in today's fast-paced world", "look no further", "we've got you
   covered", "peace of mind", "state-of-the-art", "top-notch",
   "seamless", "elevate", "unlock", "dive in", "game changer"** or any
   phrase a human technician would not say to a customer's face.
4. **Every claim is one a technician would make.** If you would not say
   it standing in the customer's garage, do not write it.
5. **Never contradict the price list.** Prices below are the only prices
   on the site. Every article, city page, FAQ and product page uses them.
6. **Every page opens with a quotable answer** that stands alone out of
   context. An answer engine lifting one paragraph must get something
   correct and complete.
7. **Numbers are specific.** "$150 to $350 per spring" not "affordable".
   "10,000 cycles, about 7 to 10 years" not "a long time".
8. **Florida, not New York.** No snow, no cold snaps, no Long Island, no
   brownstones, no stoops, no basements. Every local claim is about the
   place named. If you do not know something true about a city, say
   something true about its county or its housing era instead of
   inventing a detail.
9. **Say "we" and "you".** Never "our team of experts", never
   "customers can rest assured".
10. **Human length.** A city intro is two paragraphs of substance, not
    six of filler. Cut anything that does not tell the reader something.

The New York site's copy is the quality bar for structure and honesty.
Read two New York city entries and one service entry before writing a
Florida one, then write the Florida one from Florida facts.

## 3. Spanish: how the mirror reads

Latin American neutral Spanish, `usted` throughout, written by a person
for South Florida, Central Florida and Tampa readers, most of whom are
Cuban, Venezuelan, Colombian, Puerto Rican, Mexican, Dominican or
Central American. It is not a translation; it is the same facts, the
same prices and the same structure, written natively.

- "Puerta de garaje" is the neutral term. "Puerta de garage" is what
  many South Florida readers actually type, so it may appear once in an
  article body and in tags, never in a title.
- Springs: "resortes". Torsion spring: "resorte de torsión". Extension
  spring: "resorte de extensión". Opener: "motor" or "abridor"; use
  "motor de puerta de garaje" in headings because that is what people
  search, and "abridor" in body copy where it reads better.
- Cable: "cable". Track: "riel". Panel/section: "panel" or "sección".
  Rollers: "rodillos". Hinges: "bisagras". Off track: "fuera del riel".
- Hurricane-rated: "puerta de garaje reforzada para huracanes" or
  "certificada contra huracanes". Impact-rated: "resistente a impactos".
  Wind load: "carga de viento". Miami-Dade NOA stays "NOA de Miami-Dade".
- Same rules as English: no dashes, no exclamation marks, no filler.
- Prices identical to English. Dollar amounts, US formatting.
- Slugs under `/es/` keep the English slug for services, cities,
  regions, products and brands (the templates map them). Articles get a
  Spanish slug and declare `enSlug` in frontmatter.

## 5. Florida facts every writer may use

These are true. Do not extend them with things you are not sure of.

**Climate and wear.** Springs do not fail from cold here. They fail from
cycle count, and Florida doors cycle hard because the garage is the
storage room, the workshop and the second front door. Humidity and salt
air corrode springs, cables, hinges, bottom brackets and the torsion
tube, fastest within a few miles of either coast. Rust on a cable is the
most common visible warning we see. Wood doors face termites and rot.
Afternoon storms mean water at the bottom seal daily for half the year.
Summer garages reach 100°F and more, which cooks opener logic boards and
the plastic gears in older units. Power outages after storms mean the
manual release and battery backup matter more than anywhere else in the
country.

**Housing stock.** Almost every Florida house has an attached garage, no
basement, and a slab. Concrete block and stucco (CBS) construction
dominates in South Florida and on the coasts; frame construction with
stucco is common in Central Florida and newer inland subdivisions. Two
car garages with a single 16 foot by 7 foot double door are the norm;
three car garages with a double and a single are common in builds after
about 2000. Older South Florida neighborhoods (Hialeah, older Kendall,
Hollywood east of I-95, older Fort Lauderdale, St. Petersburg, older
Orlando neighborhoods) have 1950s to 1970s ranch houses with single
doors and low headroom. The great suburban waves were 1980s to 1990s
(Coral Springs, Pembroke Pines, Weston, Wellington, Boca Raton west,
Brandon, Palm Harbor) and 2000s to today (Wesley Chapel, Riverview,
Lake Nona, Clermont, Winter Garden, Cape Coral, Parkland, Lehigh Acres,
Port St. Lucie, Palm Coast). HOA and gated communities are everywhere in
the newer areas and often regulate door style and color. Golf cart
communities (The Villages and many 55 plus developments) have doors that
cycle several times a day.

**Hurricane code.** The Florida Building Code requires garage doors to
be rated for the design wind pressure at the site. Miami-Dade and
Broward counties are the High Velocity Hurricane Zone (HVHZ), where a
door needs a Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance (NOA). Elsewhere a door
needs a Florida Product Approval (an FL number). A wind-rated door is
built to resist pressure; an impact-rated door is also tested against
large missile impact and is what "hurricane door" usually means to a
homeowner. Replacing a garage door in Florida requires a building
permit, pulled by a licensed contractor, with the rating documented on
the permit. Replacing only an opener normally does not. Retrofit
bracing kits exist and have Florida Product Approval in most of the
state; in the HVHZ the accepted route is a rated door. The garage door
is the largest opening in the house, and when it fails the house
pressurizes and the roof is at risk, which is why the code treats it as
structural. Hurricane season runs 1 June to 30 November. The best time
for a tune-up or a door replacement is March to May.

**Insurance.** Florida's wind mitigation inspection (form OIR-B1-1802)
records whether openings, including the garage door, have rated
protection. A rated door can contribute to a premium credit under
Florida's mitigation discount rules. We say "may qualify" and "ask your
insurer for the credit schedule"; we never promise a dollar saving. The
My Safe Florida Home program has, when funded, offered grants toward
opening protection including garage doors; writers say "when the
program is open" rather than promising money.

**Storm work.** After a named storm, panels are bent inward, tracks are
twisted, and doors are pulled off track by pressure. We assess, brace or
secure the opening, and quote the repair or replacement in writing.
Insurance claims are the homeowner's; we provide the documentation and
photos. We do not inflate storm repairs and we say so, because that is
the complaint people have after every hurricane.

**Licensing language.** Say "licensed and insured" and "the permit is
pulled by a licensed contractor". Do not name a license number, a
license category, or a certifying body.

## 9. What the site will not say

- No street address, no "visit our showroom".
- No reviews, ratings, stars, testimonials, "5 star", "trusted by
  thousands".
- No founding year, no "family owned for generations".
- No license numbers or named certifications.
- No promise of an insurance saving in dollars.
- No claim that a bracing kit makes a door "hurricane proof". Nothing is.
- No cross links to any other site in the network. Ever.
- No generated image presented as a photo of our work.
