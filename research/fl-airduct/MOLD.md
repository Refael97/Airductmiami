# Part 1: The licensing question

## 1.1 The law in one table

Florida regulates mold assessment and mold remediation as licensed professions
under Chapter 468, Part XVI, sections 468.84 to 468.8424, administered by DBPR.
Two separate licenses exist: Mold Assessor (MRSA) and Mold Remediator (MRSR).

| Section | Subject | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| [468.8411](https://www.lawserver.com/law/state/florida/statutes/florida_statutes_468-8411) | Definitions | Defines mold, mold assessment, mold remediation, and sets the 10 square foot threshold |
| [468.841](https://law.justia.com/codes/florida/2010/TitleXXXII/chapter468/468_841.html) | Exemptions | Property owners on their own property, supervised employees, and holders of certain other licenses |
| [468.8413](https://m.flsenate.gov/Statutes/468.8413) | Examinations | Exam plus education and documented field experience |
| [468.8414](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/services/administrative-law-licensing/mold-related-services-license-requirements/) | Licensure and insurance | Includes the general liability insurance requirement |
| [468.8419](https://law.justia.com/codes/florida/2022/title-xxxii/chapter-468/part-xvi/section-468-8419/) | Prohibitions and penalties | The operative section for advertising and for the assess/remediate conflict |

## 1.2 The threshold is square footage, not dollars

There is **no dollar or contract-price threshold** in the Florida statute. The
assumption that one exists is wrong. Texas has monetary triggers in its mold
rules; Florida does not.

The threshold sits inside the definitions in
[468.8411](https://www.lawserver.com/law/state/florida/statutes/florida_statutes_468-8411):

> "Mold remediation" means the removal, cleaning, sanitizing, demolition, or
> other treatment, including preventive activities, of mold or mold-contaminated
> matter of greater than 10 square feet that was not purposely grown at that
> location.

> "Mold assessment" means a process performed by a mold assessor that includes
> the physical sampling and detailed evaluation of data obtained from a building
> history and inspection to formulate an initial hypothesis about the origin,
> identity, location, and extent of amplification of mold growth of greater than
> 10 square feet.

Two things follow, and both matter to us.

**First, the verbs are ours.** "Removal, cleaning, sanitizing, demolition, or
other treatment, including preventive activities" is the exact list of things an
air duct company does. The statute does not carve out an HVAC exception. What
takes an activity outside the definition is not the method, it is the object:
mold or mold-contaminated matter of more than 10 square feet.

**Second, 10 square feet of duct is nothing.** Ten square feet is roughly a 3 by
3 foot patch ([Florida Department of
Health](https://www.floridahealth.gov/environmental-health/mold/index.html)). A
single 8 inch round duct run has about 2.1 square feet of interior surface per
linear foot. Five feet of one run clears the threshold. A whole-house system
with mold "in the ducts" is over 10 square feet in almost every case. Any
marketing scenario in which we describe mold as having spread through the
ductwork is, by our own description, a licensed job.

The Florida Department of Health states the same threshold from the consumer
side: under about 10 square feet a homeowner can usually handle it, above that
Florida law requires licensed professionals for both the assessment and the
remediation ([DOH mold
page](https://www.floridahealth.gov/environmental-health/mold/index.html)).

## 1.3 Exemptions

[468.841](https://law.justia.com/codes/florida/2010/TitleXXXII/chapter468/468_841.html)
exempts, in substance:

| Exemption | Applies to us? |
|---|---|
| A residential property owner performing assessment or remediation on their own property | No. We work on customers' property. |
| An employee of a licensed assessor or remediator while directly supervised by that licensee | Only if we employ or subcontract a licensee. |
| A person working on property owned, leased, operated or managed by them or their employer, unless that party is in the business of doing this for the public | No. We are in the business of doing it for the public. |
| Persons acting within the scope of licenses under ch. 471 (engineers), ch. 481 pt. I (architects), ch. 482 (pest control), ch. 489 (construction contractors), part XV of ch. 468 (home inspectors), insurer representatives under ch. 626 pt. VI, manufactured housing licensees under ch. 320 | Only if we hold one of those licenses. |

The ch. 489 route is the one an HVAC company sometimes has: a licensed Class A,
B or C air conditioning contractor is acting within the scope of a ch. 489
license. **Air duct cleaning by itself is not a licensed activity in Florida**,
so a duct cleaning company that holds no contractor license has no exemption to
stand on ([Elevate Legal Services summary of the mold
framework](https://elawfirm.org/blog/florida-mold-related-services-license-compliance-defense-guide/)).

The exemption also has a sting in the tail. Every one of those license-based
exemptions is written as applying **except** when the exempt person holds
themselves out for hire to the public as a "certified mold remediator,"
"registered mold remediator," "licensed mold remediator," "mold remediator,"
"professional mold remediator," or any combination implying licensure under this
part. So even a licensed HVAC contractor loses the exemption by advertising the
title.

**Open item: does Florida Breeze hold a DBPR MRSR license, or a ch. 489
contractor license, or neither?** `src/data/business.ts` claims "licensed and
insured technicians" with no license number anywhere on the site, and
`src/data/facts.ts` sets `licensed: true`. I could not verify any license.
Everything in section 1.7 assumes the answer is "neither." If we do hold an MRSR
license, most of this becomes a disclosure problem rather than a licensing
problem, and the fix is publishing the number.

## 1.4 Activity by activity

This is the answer to the direct question. The rule to apply: is the object of
the work mold or mold-contaminated matter of more than 10 square feet, and are
we describing it that way?

| Activity | Inside or outside the license | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Air duct cleaning, routine dust and debris | **Outside** | Not mold-contaminated matter. Duct cleaning is a non-licensed activity in Florida. |
| HVAC coil, blower and air handler cleaning | **Outside**, as maintenance | Same. A coil is cleaned as a maintenance item, not as remediation. Note that a coil face is well under 10 sq ft, which helps. |
| Duct sanitizing with an EPA-registered antimicrobial on clean, non-contaminated ducts | **Probably outside, but not clean** | The definition covers "sanitizing ... including preventive activities" but qualifies it as sanitizing "of mold or mold-contaminated matter of greater than 10 square feet." Applying a product to a system with no mold in it is not treatment of mold-contaminated matter. This reading is mine, not DBPR's, and I could not find a DBPR interpretation confirming it. |
| Applying antimicrobial inside ductwork we have described as mold contaminated, system-wide | **Inside** | This is "sanitizing ... of mold or mold-contaminated matter of greater than 10 square feet" almost word for word. |
| Removing visible mold growth from ducts, plenum or air handler across more than 10 sq ft | **Inside** | Core definition of mold remediation. |
| Removing and replacing mold-contaminated duct board, flex liner or insulation | **Inside** | "Removal ... demolition" of mold-contaminated matter. |
| Air or surface sampling to determine whether mold is present and how far it has spread | **Inside, assessor side** | Core definition of mold assessment. |
| Recommending that a customer call a licensed remediator, with photos | **Outside** | Identifying a problem and referring it is not performing or offering to perform. |

The clean line: **we may clean ducts and we may find mold. We may not sell the
removal of it.**

## 1.5 May an unlicensed company advertise "mold remediation," "mold removal," or "black mold removal"?

No.
[468.8419(1)](https://law.justia.com/codes/florida/2022/title-xxxii/chapter-468/part-xvi/section-468-8419/)
prohibits a person from, among other things:

> Perform **or offer to perform** any mold remediation unless the person has
> complied with the provisions of this part.

with a parallel clause for mold assessment, plus title clauses barring use of
"certified mold remediator," "registered mold remediator," "licensed mold
remediator," "mold remediator," "professional mold remediator," or any
combination, and the same list on the assessor side.

"Offer to perform" is the phrase that reaches marketing. A service page headed
"Air Duct Mold Removal & Remediation" with a price range and a quote form is an
offer to perform mold remediation. It is not a close call, and the offer sits on
the page whether or not a single job is ever booked.

The three phrases asked about:

| Phrase | Status for an unlicensed company |
|---|---|
| "Mold remediation" | Offering a statutorily defined licensed service. Prohibited. |
| "Mold removal" | Not a statutory term, but "removal ... of mold" is the first verb in the definition of mold remediation. Substance over label; a regulator reads it as the same offer. Treat as prohibited. |
| "Black mold removal" | Same as above, with the added problem that it names a species we cannot identify without sampling, which is itself the assessor's licensed act. Worse, not better. |

### Penalties

| Route | Exposure |
|---|---|
| Criminal, [468.8419](https://law.justia.com/codes/florida/2013/title-xxxii/chapter-468/part-xvi/section-468.8419) | Graduated: second degree misdemeanor for a first violation, first degree misdemeanor for a second, third degree felony for a third or subsequent, punishable under ss. 775.082, 775.083, 775.084 |
| Civil and administrative, [455.228](https://law.justia.com/codes/florida/2014/title-xxxii/chapter-455/section-455.228/) | DBPR cease and desist notice, plus a civil penalty of not less than $500 and not more than $5,000 per offense, sought through circuit court, plus costs and fees, plus investigation costs if DBPR prevails |
| Practical | DBPR investigates complaints about unlicensed activity and issues cease and desist orders; unlicensed mold work is commonly described as carrying fines up to $5,000 per violation ([Elevate Legal Services](https://elawfirm.org/blog/florida-mold-related-services-license-compliance-defense-guide/)) |

The exact subsection numbering of the penalty tiers should be confirmed against
the current-year text. Two independent retrievals returned the same three tiers,
one placing them at 468.8419(3), and a third source described it as a
first degree misdemeanor escalating to a third degree felony. The tiers are
real; which subsection holds them is unconfirmed here.

Note also that the realistic first contact is not a prosecutor. It is a
competitor who holds an MRSR license, sees a statewide site selling mold
remediation with no license number, and files a DBPR complaint. That costs
nothing to do and there are hundreds of licensed remediators in our service
area.

## 1.6 "Mold testing" and "air quality testing"

Same analysis, different license. Physical sampling and evaluation to determine
the origin, identity, location and extent of mold growth over 10 square feet is
**mold assessment**, and requires the Mold Assessor (MRSA) license. Offering it
unlicensed is prohibited by the same
[468.8419](https://law.justia.com/codes/florida/2022/title-xxxii/chapter-468/part-xvi/section-468-8419/)
clause.

Our `indoor-air-quality-testing` page currently says, in a published FAQ answer:

> "Can you test specifically for mold?" ... "Yes. We take air and surface
> samples and compare them to an outdoor baseline to detect and quantify
> elevated mold spores."

That is a description of mold assessment offered in the first person. It is the
same exposure as the remediation page, on the assessor side.

Not everything on that page is caught. Measuring humidity, temperature,
particulate counts, VOCs and CO2 is not mold assessment. Selling an air and
surface mold sampling service is.

### The conflict of interest rule

[468.8419(1)](https://law.justia.com/codes/florida/2023/468.8419) also bars the
same company from doing both sides on the same property:

- A mold assessor may not perform or offer to perform mold remediation on a
  structure on which the assessor or the assessor's company provided a mold
  assessment **within the last 12 months**.
- A mold remediator may not perform or offer to perform mold assessment on a
  structure on which the remediator or the remediator's company provided mold
  remediation within the last 12 months.

There is an exception for a certified contractor classified as a Division I
contractor under s. 489.105(3). There is also a prohibition on offering
compensation, inducement or reward to an assessor or an assessor's company for
referral of business, which rules out paying a licensed assessor a kickback for
sending us work.

The practical consequence is structural, not cosmetic: **the "we test, then we
remediate, then we verify" story on our site is a business model Florida law
does not allow**, even for a fully licensed company. Getting both licenses would
not fix it. The compliant version is that an independent licensed assessor tests
and clears, and a separate licensed remediator does the work. This is why
Florida mold jobs carry a separate $250 to $700 clearance inspection line.

## 1.7 Verdict, and exactly what changes

**Verdict.** On the assumption that Florida Breeze holds no MRSR or MRSA
license, both pages are live exposure. The mold page offers a licensed service
by name and by price. The IAQ page offers the other licensed service inside an
unlicensed-sounding wrapper. Neither is a wording nicety; both are the conduct
that 468.8419 prohibits, and the exposure exists as soon as the offer is
published.

### Stop claiming

| Currently on the site | Why it goes |
|---|---|
| Service name "Air Duct Mold Removal & Remediation", slug `mold-remediation` | Offers a licensed service |
| "Air duct mold remediation is the identification, removal, and prevention of mold growth" | Defines our own service as the statutory one |
| "We inspect and, where needed, test to confirm mold ... contain ... physically remove growth and contaminated materials, apply EPA-registered antimicrobial treatment" | Describes performing remediation and assessment |
| Quick fact "Typical price $500 to $3,000+ (severity-based)" on a mold removal page | Prices a licensed service |
| IAQ FAQ "Can you test specifically for mold? Yes. We take air and surface samples ... to detect and quantify elevated mold spores" | Offers mold assessment |
| IAQ benefit "Verify remediation: Confirm that a cleaning or mold job actually solved the problem" | Offers clearance testing, which is assessor work, and pairs it with our own remediation, which is the 12-month conflict |
| Region copy: Broward "air duct cleaning, dryer vent cleaning, and mold remediation"; Palm Beach "air duct mold removal" | Same offer, repeated in `src/data/regions.ts` |
| Services hub: "routine cleaning, mold removal, dryer vent safety" | Same offer |
| Roughly 25 blog posts and 5 Spanish posts linking to `/services/mold-remediation/` with anchor text "mold remediation" | Every one is a repeated offer |

The exposure is not one page. `grep -rn "mold-remediation" src/` returns the
service data, the icon component, the services hub, two region entries and about
30 content files across both languages. Any fix has to be a data and content
sweep, not a page edit.

### May still truthfully say

Everything below is defensible for an unlicensed duct cleaning company, and
several items are stronger sales copy than what is there now.

- "Air duct cleaning" and "HVAC and coil cleaning" to the NADCA ACR source
  removal standard, at the prices already published.
- "We camera-inspect the system and show you what is inside it, including
  anything that looks like mold growth." Looking and photographing is not
  assessment; sampling is.
- "If we find what looks like mold, you get photographs and a written note of
  where it is, and we tell you plainly that Florida requires a licensed mold
  assessor to confirm it and a licensed mold remediator to remove it."
- "Musty AC smell" as the customer-facing symptom, addressed by cleaning the
  coil, the blower and the condensate drain, which is where the smell usually
  originates and is a maintenance job by any reading.
- "We are not a licensed mold remediator. When a job needs one, we will say so
  and stop, rather than sell you a cleaning that will not fix it." This is a
  differentiator, not a weakness. It is also exactly the honest-version test in
  `docs/TRUST-AND-CLAIMS.md`.
- Educational content about mold in ducts: causes, humidity numbers, what it
  costs in Florida, how the licensing works, how to verify a remediator's
  license. Publishing information about a licensed service is not offering it.
- Duct sanitizing sold as a post-cleaning odor and microbial treatment on a
  system that has been cleaned, without describing it as mold treatment. See the
  caveat in 1.4; this is the one item where I would want the DBPR FAQ read
  directly before finalising the wording.

### Two other things noticed while grepping

1. **"Licensed & Insured" appears sitewide with no license number** (footer,
   popup, FAQ, about, every city page, `agent.json`). Duct cleaning is not a
   licensed activity in Florida. If there is no license behind the word, this is
   the "certified when no certificate exists" case that
   `docs/TRUST-AND-CLAIMS.md` already prohibits. "Insured, and every technician
   background checked" would be true and would cost nothing.
2. **The IAQ price of $200 to $500 is below the Florida market** for licensed
   assessment ($450 to $900 plus $50 to $120 per lab sample, section 2.3). If
   the page is repositioned toward referral rather than sale, the number should
   go with it, or the site publishes a price for a service it does not perform.

- **The unmodified head terms are national informational SERPs.** They are held

  guides, not by anyone selling a local job. A new Florida service page will not

### What three winners do that we do not

**[SERVPRO, Mold in Vents](https://www.servpro.com/resources/mold-remediation/mold-in-vents).**
Symptom-first, not service-first. It opens on the signs a homeowner can check
themselves: musty odor that worsens when the HVAC runs, black, green or grey
discolouration around the vent cover, allergy symptoms, condensation on windows,
reduced airflow. Then it makes the point our page buries: mold in vents is a
symptom of a moisture problem, and cleaning the mold without finding the water
does not solve it. It sits inside a mold resource library, so it inherits
internal authority from dozens of sibling pages. We have the sibling pages. We
do not have the hub.

**[BELFOR, Mold In Air Vents & Ducts](https://www.belfor.com/us/en/resources/mold-in-air-vents-ducts-guide/).**
Dated and versioned as a 2026 guide, updated May 2026. It takes explicit
positions that read as expertise rather than sales: do not use bleach or vinegar
in ducts because it worsens the contamination, only surface mold on the vent
cover is a homeowner job, anything inside the system needs a professional. It
tells the reader what not to buy. Our page tells the reader what to buy.

**[Mold in Air Ducts: Removal Cost, Signs & What Miami Homeowners Need to
Know](https://airductcleaningmiamifl.com/blog/mold-in-air-ducts-miami/).** This
is the one closest to us in kind: an air duct company, in Florida, ranking on a
cost-led blog post rather than a service page. It leads with the number ($500 to
$2,000+ for duct mold removal), gives the driver of the number in one sentence
(how far it has spread: one vent versus trunk lines, air handler and evaporator
coil), and anchors it to a local fact (Miami humidity consistently above 70%).
The page is a cost guide that happens to sell, not a service page that mentions
cost.

**[MoldVerified](https://moldverified.com/cost)** is worth studying even though
it is a directory, because it is doing the thing our compliance problem
requires. Its Florida cost page publishes real ranges per city (Jacksonville
$1,000 to $3,875, Miami $1,225 to $4,700, Orlando $1,050 to $4,000, Tampa $1,075
to $4,075), states plainly that "no quote forms, no spam, just the numbers,"
notes that South Florida runs about 10 to 12% above the state baseline, and
tells the reader that Florida law requires a state mold license for anything
over 10 square feet, then counts the license-verified companies in the market.
It monetises the licensing rule instead of tripping over it.

**What none of them do:** none explains the Florida licensing split to a
homeowner in plain language, and none tells the reader the practical
consequence, which is that the company that tests the house cannot be the
company that cleans it. That is the opening.

## 2.2 The strategic read

We are an unlicensed duct cleaning company chasing a query set that is half
national informational content and half a licensed trade we cannot sell. That is
why the cluster produces zero clicks.

The winnable version is narrower and, awkwardly, more honest:

1. Give up the unmodified head terms as money terms. They are informational and
   nationally held.
2. Own the **cost plus Florida geography** layer, which is where the SERP is
   weak and where our own position 48 already sits.
3. Own the **licensing explainer** layer, which nobody in the duct or
   restoration space is covering for consumers, and which is a genuine question
   every Florida homeowner with duct mold has to answer.
4. Convert on the part of the job we may lawfully sell: cleaning, coil and
   blower work, condensate drain, humidity control, and the referral.

## 2.4 Is duct mold a cleaning job or a remediation job?

This is the question that decides what we can honestly sell, and the authorities
answer it the same way: **it depends on the duct material, and on porous
material it is a replacement job, not a cleaning job.**

From [EPA, Should You Have the Air Ducts in Your Home
Cleaned?](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/should-you-have-air-ducts-your-home-cleaned):

- Duct cleaning is warranted when "there is substantial visible mold growth
  inside hard surface (e.g., sheet metal) ducts or on other components of your
  heating and cooling system."
- "Although a substance may look like mold, a positive determination of whether
  it is mold or not can be made only by an expert and may require laboratory
  analysis for final confirmation. For about $50, some microbiology laboratories
  can tell you whether a sample sent to them on a clear strip of sticky
  household tape is mold or simply a substance that resembles it."
- "Once fiberglass duct liner is contaminated with mold, cleaning is not
  sufficient to prevent re-growth and there are no EPA-registered biocides for
  the treatment of porous duct materials."
- "EPA, NADCA and NAIMA all recommend the replacement of wet or moldy fiber
  glass duct material." Wet or visibly moldy insulation "should be removed and
  replaced by a qualified heating and cooling system contractor."
- "Biocide should be applied only to un-insulated areas of the duct system after
  proper cleaning, if necessary to reduce the chances for re-growth of mold."
- "Controlling moisture is the most effective way to prevent biological growth
  in all types of air ducts."

From NADCA: the [ACR Standard](https://nadca.com/industry-professionals/nadca-standards-and-publications)
requires the whole air handling system to be addressed, supply and return ducts,
air handling unit, blower motor, evaporator coil, condensate pan and internal
insulation, not just the duct runs. NADCA's own membership explicitly separates
"air systems cleaning specialists" from "mold remediators" as different roles,
and the professional mold remediation standard is ANSI/IICRC S520, a different
document from ACR. NADCA does not treat duct cleaning as mold remediation.

**What follows for our copy, and it is genuinely useful:**

| Situation | Honest classification | Can we sell it? |
|---|---|---|
| Dark residue at the register that has not been identified | Unknown. EPA says only an expert can confirm, possibly with lab analysis | We can clean the register and the system as maintenance. We cannot call it mold. |
| Visible growth on sheet metal duct, under 10 sq ft | Cleaning, per EPA | Yes, as cleaning. |
| Visible growth across the system on sheet metal, over 10 sq ft | Mold remediation under Florida law | No. Refer. |
| Growth on fiberglass duct liner or duct board, any amount | Replacement. Cleaning does not work and no EPA-registered biocide exists for porous duct material | No, if it is mold. This is remediation plus HVAC replacement. |
| Biofilm and odour on the evaporator coil and in the air handler | Coil and drain cleaning, a maintenance job | Yes. This is where the musty smell usually comes from and it is the honest core of our offer. |
| Applying biocide to clean, un-insulated ducts after cleaning | Permitted by EPA guidance as a re-growth measure | Yes, as sanitizing. Not as mold treatment. See 1.4. |
| Humidity control, condensate drain, sealing leaks in a hot attic | Moisture source correction, the thing EPA calls most effective | Yes, and this is under-sold on the site. |

The uncomfortable and commercially useful truth: **most of what a homeowner
calls "mold in the air ducts" is either not mold, or is on a coil we may clean,
or is on porous liner that has to be replaced rather than cleaned.** Very little
of it is a "clean the mold out of the ducts" job. A page that says this plainly
would be more accurate than any of the four winners above, would answer the real
query, and would sell the coil cleaning and the referral without offering a
licensed service.
