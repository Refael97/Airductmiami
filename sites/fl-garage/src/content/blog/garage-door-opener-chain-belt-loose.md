## What a loose chain or belt looks like

The opener pulls the door with a chain, a belt or a screw that runs along the ceiling rail. A chain or belt needs some slack, but not much. These are the signs it has too much:

- **The chain slaps the rail** with a metallic rattle as the door starts or stops.
- **The chain hangs below the rail**, sometimes far enough to see from the car.
- **The door starts with a jerk** instead of moving off smoothly.
- **A sharp click at the motor**, especially on belt units: that is the belt jumping a tooth on the sprocket.
- **The door stops or reverses as it starts**, because the jerk trips the force sensing.

If the chain has already come off the sprocket or snapped and the motor runs without moving anything, the guide you want is [opener runs but the door does not move](/blog/opener-runs-door-doesnt-move/).

## How much slack is normal

Many chain drive manufacturers call for the chain to sit about half an inch above the base of the rail at the midpoint. Look at it with the door closed. If it rests on the rail, it is loose. If it is tight as a string and does not give under a finger, it is too tight.

Belts naturally sag less than chains, and the giveaway on a belt is more often the click on start up than slack you can see. Each brand gives its own figure, so check the manual before adjusting.

## Why it loosens

**Normal wear.** A chain stretches slightly over thousands of cycles, and a belt does too. An opener that has gone years without adjustment usually needs one.

**A heavy door.** The cause most people miss. When a spring loses tension the door weighs more than the opener expects, and every cycle stretches the chain and punishes the sprocket. Unplug the opener, pull the release and lift the door by hand to waist height. If it will not stay there, the root problem is the spring, and [spring replacement](/services/garage-door-spring-replacement/) runs $150 to $350 per spring.

**Heat and salt.** A Florida garage passes 100°F in summer and the rail sits in the hottest air in the room. Near the coast, salt air rusts chain links until they stiffen and kink, as we cover in [salt air garage door corrosion](/blog/salt-air-garage-door-corrosion/).

**A loose rail.** If the header bracket above the door or the ceiling hanger has worked loose, the whole rail shifts on every start and it feels like a slack chain. Tighten those first.

## How tension is adjusted

On most units the adjustment sits at the trolley, the carriage that runs along the rail and connects to the door arm, or at the door end of the rail. There is a threaded rod with two nuts.

1. Close the door and unplug the opener.
2. Loosen the outer nut, the one that locks the setting.
3. Turn the inner nut a little at a time to take up slack.
4. Measure at the midpoint and stop at the figure in the manual.
5. Lock the outer nut, plug the opener back in and run the door two or three times.

**Do not overtighten.** A chain that is too tight wears the sprocket and the motor bearings and makes more noise, not less. While you are on the ladder, leave the springs and cables alone.

Afterwards, a chain gets a light coat of lubricant along its length. A belt gets nothing. Our guide on [how to lubricate a garage door](/blog/how-to-lubricate-a-garage-door/) lists what goes on each part.

## When adjustment is not enough

- The threaded rod has no thread left to take up.
- The chain has stiff, kinked or rusted links that will not straighten.
- The belt has worn teeth, cracks or exposed cords.
- The motor sprocket teeth are hooked or missing.
- The rail is bent and the chain rubs at one spot.

Any of those means a new part, and on an older unit it is worth pricing a new opener alongside. Our comparison of [belt and chain drive openers](/blog/belt-vs-chain-drive-opener/) covers the choice.
