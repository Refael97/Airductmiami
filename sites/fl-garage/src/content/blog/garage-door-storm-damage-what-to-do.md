## What storm damage looks like

Wind does three things to a garage door, and after a named storm we see all three in the same neighborhood.

**Panels bent inward.** Pressure on the outside of the door bows the sections toward the garage. On a light door the top and middle sections crease along a horizontal line. On a heavier door the bow may be slight, a few inches at the center, and the door still moves. Both are damage.

**Twisted or pulled track.** The vertical tracks take the load when the door tries to lift, and they bend, pull their lag screws out of the jamb, or spread apart. A door on a spread track runs for a cycle or two, then a roller pops out.

**Door pulled off track by pressure.** The door lifts, a roller leaves the rail, and the section hangs. Sometimes the whole door drops back crooked. Cables slack and come off the drums on one side, which unloads a spring unevenly. This is the one that hurts people, and it is the one not to touch.

Water and debris get into the tracks as well. Sand in a bottom roller and salt water on a spring both matter, but they are the second problem, not the first.

## First hour: do not run it

If the door is damaged in any of those ways, do not press the opener button. Not to test it. The opener will try to lift a door that is bound in the track, and it will strip its gear, crease a bowed section further, or pull a section off the rollers entirely. Every attempt makes the repair bigger.

Do not pull the manual release on a door that is off track or hanging. The cables and springs are holding it in a way they were not designed for, and releasing the trolley can let the door move.

If the door is closed and holding, leave it closed. If it is open and cannot be closed, do not stand under it and do not park under it.

## Photograph everything before anyone touches it

This is the step people skip because they want the door dealt with. Take the photographs first. Adjusters want to see the damage as the storm left it, not after a technician has straightened the track.

| Photo | Why |
|---|---|
| Outside, full door, straight on | Shows the bow and any creases |
| Outside, from the side, low | Shows how far the door is pushed in |
| Inside, full door with the tracks | Shows track, rollers, cables and any separation |
| Each damaged section, close | Creases, cracked stiles, broken hinges |
| Each track from the floor to the curve | Bent track, pulled screws, spread |
| The cables and drums | Slack, frayed or off the drum |
| The opener and rail | Bent rail, damaged trolley |
| The label on the door | Model and rating, if readable |
| Debris that hit the door | Branch, tile, neighbor's fence |

Take them in daylight if you can, and take more than you think you need. Then keep them. A claim can take weeks, and a photograph that is easy to find in October is worth something.

## Secure the opening

Most policies expect you to prevent further damage once the storm is over. For a garage door, that means the opening is closed off from rain and from entry, and the door cannot fall.

If the door is down and holding, lock it. Engage the slide lock, or clamp the track just above a roller on each side with locking pliers. Then leave it.

If the door is open, off track, or hanging, we secure it. That means bracing the sections, clamping the tracks, and if needed lowering the door under control and locking it, or covering the opening with plywood if the door cannot be made safe. The assessment and the securing are the $150 starting point on [storm damage repair](/services/storm-damage-garage-door-repair/). You get a written assessment the same visit, with photographs, that you can hand to your adjuster.

## Repair or replace

A door with one creased section and straight tracks is a repair, if the manufacturer still makes that profile. A door with two or more creased sections, spread tracks and a bent opener rail is usually a replacement, because the panel cost alone approaches a new door and the new door is rated.

The rating is the reason replacement often makes sense after a storm. A door that bowed in was not up to the pressure at your site, or was rated for it once and has aged out. A replacement in Florida must be rated for the design pressure at the site and needs a [permit pulled by a licensed contractor](/blog/garage-door-permit-florida/). In [Miami-Dade](/service-areas/miami-dade-county/) and [Broward](/service-areas/broward-county/) that means a door with a Miami-Dade NOA. Elsewhere, including [Southwest Florida](/service-areas/southwest-florida/), it means a Florida Product Approval. We go through the decision in [repair or replace](/blog/repair-or-replace-garage-door/).

After a storm, rated doors are also in short supply. We tell you the lead time when we quote and we do not promise a door we cannot get. Manufacturer lead times after a major storm are covered in [the best time to replace a garage door in Florida](/blog/best-time-to-replace-garage-door-florida/).

## The claim is yours; the paperwork is ours

Insurance claims belong to the homeowner. We do not file them, we do not negotiate them, and we do not tell you what your policy covers, because we do not know your policy.

What we do is give you what the adjuster asks for: a written assessment with the cause of damage as we found it, dated photographs, an itemized quote for repair and, if relevant, an itemized quote for replacement. If the adjuster wants to inspect before permanent work, we can wait, with the opening secured. If the adjuster accepts photographs and a quote, we proceed when you say so.

## Storm chasers

After a hurricane, companies you have never heard of arrive from out of state and work the damaged streets. Some are fine. The pattern to watch for is the one behind most complaints: a knock on the door, a price that is only good today, cash, no written quote, no permit, and a company that is gone by the time the door fails.

Ask three things. Is the quote in writing. Who pulls the permit. Where were you working in April. A local licensed and insured company answers all three without pausing.
