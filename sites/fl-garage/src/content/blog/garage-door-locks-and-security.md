## First, the mistake that costs the most

Somebody throws the manual slide lock across, usually before going away. Somebody else comes home and presses the remote.

The opener pulls with several hundred pounds of force. The door is bolted to its track and cannot move. Something has to give, and it is usually several things at once: the bottom panel bends, a strut tears free, the opener arm bends or snaps, the drive gear strips.

**This is one of the most expensive avoidable repairs in the trade**, and it is entirely a communication failure inside a household.

If you have an automatic opener, you do not need a manual lock, and the safest thing you can do is remove the slide locks entirely or bolt them permanently in the open position. If you keep them, put a note on the wall control, and make sure everyone in the house knows.

## Your opener already is a lock

This surprises people, so it is worth being clear.

When a garage door is closed and connected to its opener, the trolley on the rail holds it down. The door cannot be lifted from outside without breaking that connection or defeating the opener, and there is no handle or keyhole to attack.

Adding a mechanical lock to a door with a working opener does not improve security. It only adds a way to destroy the door.

## The locks that do exist, and where each belongs

**Slide lock, also called a side lock or cane bolt.** A steel bar that slides through the track. Genuinely secure, genuinely simple, and correct for a **manual door with no opener**. Wrong for anything with an opener connected.

**T-handle lock.** The turning handle in the middle of the door, with a key, connected by cables to bolts on each side. Common on older manual doors. Same rule applies: fine without an opener, a hazard with one.

**Opener lock or vacation mode.** A button on the wall console that disables every remote and keypad while the wall button carries on working. **This is the right lock for a door with an opener.** It has all of the benefit and none of the risk. Use it when you go away.

**Automatic deadbolt.** Some higher end openers, particularly commercial and some LiftMaster residential models, have a motorised deadbolt that throws into the track when the door closes and retracts before it opens. This is the only mechanical lock that is safe with an opener, because the opener controls it.

**Padlock through the track.** Same as a slide lock, same rules, and it also chews the track. Use a proper slide lock if you need one.

## Where garages actually get broken into

Worth knowing, because it is rarely the main door.

**The service door.** The side or rear pedestrian door into the garage is usually the weakest point on the whole building: a hollow core slab, a builder grade lock, a strike plate held by half inch screws, and no view from the street. **Fix this first.** A solid door, a proper deadbolt and three inch screws into the framing cost very little and change the picture completely.

**The door into the house.** Enormous numbers of people leave this unlocked because the garage feels like inside. It is not inside. It is the first thing to lock and the last thing anyone does.

**The emergency release cord.** On some older doors there is enough gap at the top for a wire hook to reach the release lever from outside. A release shield, or a zip tie through the lever that still breaks under a hard pull, closes this. It costs almost nothing.

**Windows in the door.** More a matter of what people can see than how they get in. A garage with a visible workshop or a visible motorcycle is a garage worth a second look. Frosted film solves it.

**The remote in a car on the street.** A remote clipped to a visor in an unlocked car parked outside is a key to your house sitting in public. Take it inside, or use a keypad and no remote at all.

## What we would actually recommend, in order

1. **Lock the door between the garage and the house.** Free, and it is the difference between losing a bicycle and losing everything.
2. **Upgrade the service door lock and strike plate.** Under a hundred dollars of hardware and it addresses the real weak point.
3. **Take remotes out of cars parked outside.** Free.
4. **Use vacation mode when you travel.** Free, already on your wall control.
5. **Shield or tie the emergency release** if there is a visible gap at the top of the door. A few dollars.
6. **Consider a smart opener** if you want to know the door is shut. The most common way a garage gets entered is that it was left open, and an opener that tells you it is open at eleven at night solves a real problem rather than a theoretical one.

## Old openers are the security problem people miss

If your opener predates roughly 1993 it almost certainly uses a **fixed code** set with dip switches inside the remote. There were only a few hundred or a few thousand combinations.

That means a neighbour with the same brand and the same switch positions can open your door from their own driveway without ever knowing. It also means the code can be recorded and replayed with cheap equipment.

Every opener made since uses a rolling code that changes on every press, which closes that hole completely. If you open your remote and find a row of tiny switches, no lock you add to the door addresses the actual vulnerability, and our guide on [doors that open by themselves](/blog/garage-door-opens-by-itself/) covers what to do about it.

## If you have just moved house

Wipe the opener memory. Hold the Learn button on the motor unit for six seconds and every stored remote and keypad is erased, then reprogram only the remotes physically in your hand.

You have no idea how many remotes the previous owner handed out over the years, or which contractors and cleaners still have one. This takes ten minutes and it is the single best security action available to a new homeowner. The steps are in our guide on [programming a garage door remote](/blog/how-to-program-a-garage-door-remote/).
