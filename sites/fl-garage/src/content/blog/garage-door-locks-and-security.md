## First, the mistake that costs the most

Somebody throws the manual slide lock across, usually before going away. Somebody else comes home and presses the remote.

The opener pulls with several hundred pounds of force. The door is bolted to its track and cannot move. Something has to give, and it is usually several things at once: the bottom panel bends, a strut tears free, the opener arm bends or snaps, the drive gear strips.

This is one of the most expensive avoidable repairs in the trade, and it is entirely a communication failure inside a household. We see it most in seasonal homes, where one person closes the house up in May and a different person opens it in November.

If you have an automatic opener, you do not need a manual lock, and the safest thing you can do is remove the slide locks or bolt them permanently open. If you keep them, put a note on the wall control, and make sure everyone in the house knows.

## Your opener already is a lock

When a garage door is closed and connected to its opener, the trolley on the rail holds it down. The door cannot be lifted from outside without breaking that connection or defeating the opener, and there is no handle or keyhole to attack.

Adding a mechanical lock to a door with a working opener does not improve security. It only adds a way to destroy the door.

## The locks that exist, and where each belongs

| Lock | What it is | Correct use | With an opener connected |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slide lock, side lock, cane bolt | Steel bar through the track | Manual door with no opener | Never |
| T-handle | Keyed turning handle with cables to bolts each side | Older manual doors | Never |
| Padlock through the track | Same as a slide lock, and it chews the track | Manual door, and a proper slide lock is better | Never |
| Opener lock or vacation mode | Button on the wall console that disables remotes and keypads | Any door with an opener | Yes, this is the right lock |
| Automatic deadbolt | Motorized bolt some higher end openers throw into the track on close | Doors with those openers | Yes, the opener controls it |

The pattern is simple. A mechanical lock belongs on a door with no motor. A door with a motor is locked by the motor.

## Open garages in gated communities

This is the security problem we actually see, and it is not a lock problem.

In Weston, Boca Raton west of the Turnpike, Wesley Chapel, Lakewood Ranch and a hundred communities like them, the gate creates a feeling that the neighborhood is inside and the garage can stay open. So it does, all afternoon, with bikes, golf clubs, a golf cart, a workshop and the door into the house on view. Then a landscaping crew, a delivery driver or a teenager from three streets over walks in.

The fixes are behavior and one piece of hardware:

- **Close the door when you are not in the garage.** The gate is not a lock.
- **A timer to close.** Most openers made in the last decade can be set to close the door automatically after a set number of minutes. Ask us to enable it, or ask on your next [tune-up](/services/garage-door-tune-up/).
- **A smart opener.** It tells your phone the door has been open for an hour, and closes it from wherever you are. [Smart opener installation](/services/smart-garage-door-opener-installation/) is $350 to $750, and our guide on [whether a smart opener is worth it](/blog/smart-garage-door-opener-worth-it/) covers what else you get.

## Where garages actually get broken into

**The service door.** The side door into the garage is usually the weakest door on the house: a hollow slab, a builder grade lock, a strike plate held by half inch screws, and no view from the street. Fix this first. A solid door, a proper deadbolt and three inch screws into the framing change the picture completely.

**The door into the house.** Enormous numbers of people leave this unlocked because the garage feels like inside. It is not inside.

**The emergency release cord.** On some older doors there is enough gap at the top for a wire hook to reach the release lever from outside. A release shield, or a zip tie through the lever that still breaks under a hard pull, closes this.

**Windows in the door.** More about what people can see than how they get in. Frosted film solves it.

**The remote in a car in the driveway.** A remote clipped to a visor in an unlocked car is a key to your house sitting outside. Take it in, or use a keypad and no remote at all.

## Storm season and the lock question

Every June we get asked whether to lock the garage door before a storm. The honest answer is that a slide lock does nothing against wind. The pressure on a 16 foot door in a hurricane is measured in thousands of pounds, and it acts on the panels, not on the track bolt. What resists it is a wind-rated or impact-rated door, or on an older door a bracing kit with Florida Product Approval. Our guides on [preparing your garage door for a hurricane](/blog/prepare-garage-door-for-hurricane/) and [hurricane bracing kits](/blog/garage-door-hurricane-bracing-kits/) cover what actually helps.

For security while you are gone: close the door, press the lock button on the wall control, take the remotes with you, and leave the slide lock alone. If the power fails, the trolley still holds the door. When you come back, the door opens normally and nothing has been bent.

## Old openers are the security problem people miss

If your opener predates roughly 1993 it almost certainly uses a fixed code set with dip switches inside the remote. There were only a few hundred or a few thousand combinations, so a neighbor with the same brand and the same switch positions can open your door from their own driveway without knowing, and the code can be recorded and replayed with cheap equipment.

Every opener made since uses a rolling code that changes on every press. If you open your remote and find a row of tiny switches, no lock you add to the door addresses the real vulnerability. Our guides on a [door that opens by itself](/blog/garage-door-opens-by-itself/) and on [how old is too old for an opener](/blog/old-garage-door-opener-safety/) cover what to do.

## If you have just moved, or just come back

Wipe the opener memory. Hold the Learn button on the motor unit until the light goes out and every stored remote and keypad is erased, then reprogram only the remotes in your hand. The steps are in our guide on [programming a garage door remote](/blog/how-to-program-a-garage-door-remote/).

You have no idea how many remotes the previous owner handed out. Seasonal residents should do the same when they return in November, because a summer of pool service, pest control and contractors has passed through the house.
