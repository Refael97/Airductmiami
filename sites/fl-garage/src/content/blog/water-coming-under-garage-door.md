## Find where it is getting in

Do this before buying anything, because the answer depends entirely on where the water enters.

Close the door on a sunny day, turn the garage lights off and look along the bottom from inside. Daylight along the whole edge, or in one corner, is your gap.

Then get someone to run a hose along the bottom of the door from outside while you watch from inside. Note the pattern.

- **Even across the whole width:** the bottom seal.
- **One spot, usually toward the middle:** the slab or the driveway.
- **One corner:** the door is closing crooked.
- **The sides:** the jamb weatherstripping, or the door not sitting square against it.

## The bottom seal, first suspect

The bottom seal is the rubber or vinyl strip along the bottom edge of the door that squashes into the slab. A concrete floor is never perfectly flat, and the seal is what fills the difference.

Florida is hard on it. Afternoon sun on a south or west facing door bakes the rubber until it stops being flexible, and a seal that cannot squash cannot seal. Daily heat cycling then cracks it along the fold. Three to five years is a normal life here, and on a door that faces the afternoon sun it is often the short end of that.

Look for rubber that is hard, cracked, split, or pulled out of the retainer at one end. Palmetto bugs, ants and lizards getting in with the door shut are the same gap.

This is a genuine homeowner job. Our [bottom seal parts page](/parts/bottom-seal/) explains how. The one catch is the aluminum retainer the seal slides into. If it is bent, or pitted with corrosion, which is common within a few miles of the coast, a new seal will not seat and the retainer needs doing too.

## When the floor is the problem

Florida slabs settle. A slab that has developed a shallow dip leaves the seal bridging a gap it was never sized for, and water finds that one spot every time. A driveway that has settled so it now drains toward the garage instead of away from it does the same thing on a larger scale, with water pooling right against the door.

A new seal alone will not solve either one. The usual answer is a threshold strip, a raised rubber profile bonded to the slab under the closed door. The door seal lands on it and water running across the driveway meets a curb. We quote it as a part.

## When the door is the problem

**It closes crooked.** If water comes in at one corner, look at whether one side of the door reaches the floor before the other. That is usually a cable that has slipped or a spring that has lost tension, and it is not something to level yourself. Our guide on [a crooked or uneven garage door](/blog/garage-door-crooked-uneven/) covers it.

**It stops short.** A door that stops an inch above the slab has a down travel limit set too short. That is adjusted during a tune-up. The opposite fault, a limit set too far, makes the door reverse as soon as it touches down, and is covered in [a door that closes then reopens](/blog/garage-door-closes-then-reopens/).

**The sides leak.** The jamb weatherstripping fails from sunlight too, and the side that gets the afternoon sun goes years before the shaded side. Our [weatherstripping page](/parts/weather-seal/) explains what to check.

## What standing water does to the door

The bottom brackets, where the lift cables attach, sit a couple of inches above that wet slab. They are the first part of the door to rust, and a cable that has gone brown and fuzzy near the bracket will break within months. Cable repair is $95 to $300; our guide on [cable repair cost](/blog/garage-door-cable-repair-cost/) explains what changes the price. Wood doors rot from the bottom panel up.

The photo eye wire runs along the wall at floor level too, and water sitting against it corrodes the terminals so the sensors drop out on humid days. Our guide on [rust from Florida humidity](/blog/garage-door-rust-florida-humidity/) goes further.

## What a seal cannot do

A seal is for rain. If the street floods or storm surge comes in, water enters the garage seal or no seal, and no garage door is designed to stop it.

An impact-rated door does not promise a dry floor either. Its job is to stay in the opening under wind pressure. Our guide to [preparing a garage door for a hurricane](/blog/prepare-garage-door-for-hurricane/) covers what to do before a storm.
