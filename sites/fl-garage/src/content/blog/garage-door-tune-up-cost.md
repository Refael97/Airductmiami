## What it should include

A tune-up that costs $95 and takes twenty minutes is worth what you paid. Here is the full list of what a real one covers.

**Balance test.** Disconnect the opener, lift the door halfway by hand, and let go. It should stay put. If it drops or rises, the spring is not matched to the door and everything else is downstream of that.

**Cables and drums.** Check for fraying at the drum, rust at the bottom bracket, and even tension on both sides.

**Rollers.** Check every roller turns freely. Seized rollers drag, wear the track, and eventually derail the door.

**Hinges and brackets.** Check for cracks, particularly at the end hinges which carry the roller load.

**Hardware.** A garage door vibrates every time it runs. Bolts work loose. Tightening them is unglamorous and it prevents a lot.

**Track alignment.** Check the track is plumb, evenly spaced and firmly fixed along its full length.

**Lubrication.** Springs, hinges, rollers, bearings and the opener rail. Not the track itself, which should be clean and dry.

**Safety sensors.** Check alignment and mounting height. They belong no more than six inches above the floor, which is the height at which they will actually catch a child or a pet.

**Opener force and travel.** Check the door reverses on contact and that the travel limits stop it in the right places.

**Weather seal.** Check the bottom seal is intact and sitting flat.

## Why it is the best value item on the list

Almost every emergency call we take has a cheaper version of itself that happened months earlier and was ignored.

A grinding noise is a dry roller bearing, which is a $10 part. Left alone, it seizes, drags in the track, and takes the door off it. That is now a $150 to $700 job.

A slightly frayed cable is a $225 replacement. Left alone, it snaps, the door drops on one side, and the repair triples.

A door that has gone slightly out of balance is a spring adjustment. Left alone, the opener compensates for months and then burns out, and that is $281 minimum.

## Timing it

The most useful time to have this done in New York is autumn, before the first hard freeze. Cold weather is what finishes off springs that are already near the end of their cycle life, and it is what thickens the grease in older openers.

A door that gets checked in October is far less likely to fail on the coldest morning in January, which happens to be the morning you least want to be standing in the driveway with a car you cannot get out.
