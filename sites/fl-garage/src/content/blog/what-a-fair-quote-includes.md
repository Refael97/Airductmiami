## The nine items

**1. The total price of the finished repair.** Not the visit, not the call-out, not a starting figure. What you will pay when the job is done.

**2. Parts and labor, separately.** You should be able to see what proportion is which. This matters when comparing two quotes that arrive at similar totals by different routes. Where we quote labor hourly it is $45 to $120 per hour, and most repairs are quoted as a job rather than by the hour.

**3. The part specification.** For a spring: wire size, inside diameter, length, and cycle rating. For an opener: brand, model, drive type, and whether battery backup is included. For a door: material, gauge, insulation R-value and size. Without these you are not comparing anything.

**4. Any call-out or trip fee, stated separately.** And whether it comes off the repair price if you go ahead. The [emergency premium](/services/emergency-garage-door-repair/) for same-day and after-hours dispatch is $150 to $300 and it belongs on the quote before the truck leaves.

**5. The warranty, on parts and on labor, as separate numbers.** "We stand behind our work" is not a warranty. One year parts, one year labor, printed on the invoice, is.

**6. An arrival window.** A few hours, not a day, and a commitment to call if it slips.

**7. What happens if more is found.** Work stops, you get a new number, nothing proceeds until you agree. In writing.

**8. For a new door: the wind rating, the approval number and who pulls the permit.** This is the Florida item, and it is where door quotes most often fall short. The quote should say what design pressure the door is rated for, whether it is wind-rated or impact-rated, the Florida Product Approval number or Miami-Dade NOA it carries, and that the contractor pulls the permit. If the permit is a separate line, that is fine. If it is absent, ask.

**9. It exists in writing before work starts.** All of the above, sent to you.

## What each one protects you from

| Item | What it protects you from |
|---|---|
| Total finished price | The bait number that becomes a bigger one on the driveway |
| Parts and labor split | Two quotes that look alike but are not |
| Part specification | Paying for a 20,000 cycle spring and receiving a 10,000 cycle one |
| Separate call-out fee | Paying twice for the same visit |
| Warranty in numbers | A promise that evaporates with the phone number |
| Arrival window | Losing a day waiting |
| Found more clause | An agreed price that becomes an opening bid |
| Rating and permit | An unpermitted door that does nothing at inspection or claim time |
| In writing | All of the above |

The specification protects you from paying for a high-cycle spring and receiving a standard one, which you would never know from looking at it.

The separate call-out fee protects you from paying twice for the same visit.

The written "what if we find more" clause protects you from the single most common version of the bait pattern: an agreed price that becomes an opening bid once the door is apart.

The rating and permit item protects you from the Florida version of the same problem. A door installed without a permit has no inspection, no rating on record, nothing to show at a wind mitigation inspection, and a warranty that depends on finding the installer again. After a storm, that is the door the insurer questions.

The written estimate protects you from all of it, which is why it is the item some companies will not provide.

## What a spring quote looks like when it is right

For the most common job in Florida, a broken torsion spring on a standard double door, a fair quote reads something like this: one spring, wire size and length stated, 10,000 cycle rating, $150 to $350 installed, with the second spring at the same visit for $120 to $250 more because it has taken the same cycles and will go next. Cable checked, and if rusted, quoted separately at $95 to $300. One year parts, one year labor. Arrival window given. No call-out fee.

Everything on that list is on our [spring replacement page](/services/garage-door-spring-replacement/), and our article on [spring replacement cost](/blog/garage-door-spring-replacement-cost/) explains where in the range a given door lands.

## What a door quote looks like when it is right

Material, gauge and insulation named. Size. Design pressure and whether the door is wind-rated or impact-rated. Approval number. Springs sized for the new door, because a rated door is heavier than the one it replaces and reusing the old spring is the most common installation mistake we see. Opener compatibility. Permit pulled by the contractor. Old door disposal. Lead time. Price: $950 to $4,200 for a [wind-rated steel single](/services/new-garage-door-installation/), $1,800 to $6,500 for a [hurricane-rated door](/services/hurricane-rated-garage-door-installation/), permit included in both.

If any of those lines is missing, the quote is incomplete rather than dishonest, and the fix is to ask. If the company cannot supply the missing line, that is a different conversation.

## What our quote looks like

The published starting price is on the site before you call. When you describe the problem, you get a range for that specific job. If you go ahead, you get a written estimate before anything is touched, and that number is what the invoice says.

If we find something you did not know about, work stops and you get a new number. If you say no at that point, you owe us for what has been done and nothing more.

On a door, the rating, the approval number and the permit are on the estimate, because in Florida they are the job.

None of that is generous. It is what a quote is supposed to be.

## The question that reveals the most

"Can you send me that in writing before you come out?"

It costs an honest company nothing. It costs a dishonest one their entire method. The answer you get tells you almost everything you need to know about how the visit will go.
