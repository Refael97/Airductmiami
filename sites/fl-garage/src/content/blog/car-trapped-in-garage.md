## Check the power before anything else

In Florida the most common reason a door will not open with a car behind it is not the door. It is an opener with no power. An afternoon storm takes the power out, or the surge when it comes back trips the GFCI outlet the opener is plugged into, and the door sits there doing nothing while you press the button.

Look at the opener light. Press the wall button. If there is no light and no sound, the opener is dead and the door itself is probably fine. That is the easy version of this problem: pull the release, lift the door by hand, and drive out. Openers with a battery backup keep working through an outage, which is why we recommend one on every install here. Our guide to [battery backup and power outages](/blog/garage-door-battery-backup-power-outage/) covers that.

If the opener has power and runs but the door does not move, or it hums and strains, keep reading, because now the door itself is the question.

## Look at the spring before you touch anything

Stand inside with the door closed and look at the shaft above the opening. A torsion spring that has broken has a visible gap in the coil, usually an inch or two wide, with the two halves sitting apart on the shaft. If you heard a loud bang earlier in the day or overnight, that was probably it.

![A snapped garage door torsion spring showing the gap where the coil separated. A door with this behind it weighs everything it is built from, and the opener cannot lift it.](/images/broken-torsion-spring.webp)

Florida springs do not break from cold. They break from cycle count, and a Florida garage door cycles hard because it is the front door for most of the family, the way to the storage and the workshop, and in golf cart communities it can run several times a day. A standard spring is rated for about 10,000 cycles, which at four cycles a day is around seven years. Humidity and salt air within a few miles of either coast shorten that by pitting the steel. Our guide on [how long springs last](/blog/how-long-do-garage-door-springs-last/) goes into the numbers.

If the springs look intact, the problem is more likely the opener, and the door will lift normally by hand once it is disconnected.

## If the springs are intact

1. Make sure the door is fully closed.
2. Pull the red manual release handle down and back toward the motor. It should click as the trolley disconnects.
3. Lift the door by hand. With working springs it should feel like twenty or thirty pounds, not two hundred.
4. It should stay up on its own. If it does, drive out.
5. Close it by hand, slowly, and leave the opener disconnected until it is fixed. There is no spring problem here, but a door coming down fast still hurts.

Our step by step on [using the manual release](/blog/garage-door-manual-release-how-to/) explains which cord to pull if you are not sure.

## If a spring has broken

This is the situation that needs care, because there is nothing holding the door up except you.

Most Florida garages have a 16 foot by 7 foot double door, and a double steel door with no spring support is 200 to 350 pounds. A single is 130 to 200. Our article on [how much a garage door weighs](/blog/how-much-does-a-garage-door-weigh/) explains why an insulated or hurricane-rated door sits at the top of those ranges.

1. **Do not use the opener.** Not once, not to see if it helps.
2. Get a second person. Two people can lift a single door with a broken spring. A double door is a three person job, and if you do not have three, stop here.
3. Lift together, evenly, hands flat on the bottom section and clear of the joints between sections. Fingers get caught in section joints, which is a real and common injury.
4. **Prop it.** Not with a broom. Use something that will hold that weight if you let go: a pair of stepladders, or locking pliers clamped onto the track below the bottom rollers on both sides.
5. Move the car.
6. Bring the door back down slowly, everybody together, and leave it closed.

If you cannot lift it, stop. Trying harder is how people get hurt. Call for [emergency service](/services/emergency-garage-door-repair/) and say the car is trapped, because it changes the priority. Our guide on [what to do with a broken spring](/blog/what-to-do-broken-spring/) covers the rest of that day.

## What not to do

**Do not run the opener.** It will strain the motor, crease the top section, and it can pull the door off the track. It also turns a $150 to $350 spring job into a spring, panel and off-track repair costing several times that.

**Do not stand under a propped door** any longer than you have to.

**Do not touch the springs, the cables or the bottom brackets.** The bottom bracket in particular is under full cable tension even when a spring has broken, and it is one of the most dangerous parts on the door. In coastal areas the bracket bolts are often rusted, which makes them more likely to let go if disturbed, not less.

**Do not leave it propped** and use the garage normally around it. Get the car out, bring the door down, and keep it closed until it is repaired.
