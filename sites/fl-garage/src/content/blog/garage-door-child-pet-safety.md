## Why this matters more in a Florida garage

In most of the country the garage is where the car sleeps. Here it is the second front door. Children come and go through it, bikes and scooters live in it, the dog naps on the cool slab in the shade of the opening, and the door cycles six to ten times a day instead of twice.

More cycles mean more moments when a small person or an animal is in the opening while the door is moving. The three protections below are the same everywhere. The number of times a day they get tested for real is higher here.

## The three protections

**Photo eye sensors.** An infrared beam across the opening, six inches or less above the floor. Break it while the door is closing and the door reverses. We explain how they work and how to align them in our guide to [garage door safety sensors](/blog/garage-door-safety-sensors-explained/).

**Force reversal.** The opener monitors how hard it is working. If it meets more resistance than its setting allows, it stops and reverses. This is what catches whatever the beam misses.

**Physical placement.** The wall control high enough that a child cannot reach it, and positioned so the door is visible from it.

All three exist because garage doors have killed children. Federal rules made an entrapment protection system mandatory on residential openers made after January 1993.

## The five minute test

| Test | How | Pass | Fail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sensor test | With the door open, press close and pass an object through the beam near the floor | Door reverses at once | Door keeps closing |
| Reversal test | Lay a piece of two by four flat on the floor in the opening and close the door onto it | Door touches, stops, reverses | Door pushes and stalls |
| Wall control | Measure from the floor to the button | Five feet or more | A child can reach it |
| Release cord | Measure from the floor to the handle | Six feet or more | It hangs lower |

Use an object for the sensor test, never a hand or a foot. Do both moving tests twice a year, alongside the balance test in our [maintenance checklist](/blog/garage-door-maintenance-checklist/). April or May, before storm season, is a good time for one of them, because that is also when a [tune-up](/services/garage-door-tune-up/) does the most good.

## The mistakes that disable all of this

**Sensors mounted too high.** The most common one. Somebody mounts them at knee height because it is easier, and a toddler lying down or a dog asleep in the opening is below the beam entirely.

**Force turned up to stop nuisance reversals.** A door that reverses for no obvious reason is annoying, and the tempting fix is turning up the force. That setting is the only thing stopping the door from crushing what is under it. Find out why it is reversing instead. Our guide on a [door that closes then reopens](/blog/garage-door-closes-then-reopens/) covers the usual causes.

**Sensors bypassed after a sun glare problem.** West facing Florida garages lose the beam to low afternoon sun, the door refuses to close at six in the evening, and someone taps the sensors up out of the way or wires around them. The glare has a fix that takes ten minutes. Removing the sensors is not it.

**Hold to close used as a habit.** Most openers close the door if you hold the wall button down continuously, which is meant for a one-off when a sensor has failed. Some households use it daily. Every time, the door is closing with no protection at all.

**A wall button within reach.** A three year old who can reach the button can operate a two hundred pound door.

**A manual release cord hanging low.** Children pull them. On a door with a broken spring, pulling the release with the door up lets it fall. Our guide to the [manual release](/blog/garage-door-manual-release-how-to/) covers where it should hang and when it is safe to use.

## Pets, specifically

Dogs are the most common animal we hear about, and the pattern is always the same: the dog is lying across the threshold in the shade, someone presses the remote from the car, and the dog either does not move or moves late. Working sensors at the right height are what catch that.

Cats climb. A cat on top of the door sections or in the track gets carried up into the opener rail or pinched between sections. If you have a cat that spends time in the garage, look before you press the button.

And on heat: a closed Florida garage in July is over 100°F by early afternoon. It is not a safe place to leave an animal for the day, whatever the door is doing.

## What to teach

That the garage door is not a toy, that nobody stands or plays under it, and that only adults press the button.

For older children with their own remote: the door is watched until it is fully closed, and nobody runs under a closing door. The habit of ducking under a door on its way down is the one we would most like to see end.

## When to replace rather than adjust

An opener manufactured before 1993 has no photo eye sensors, and there is no adjustment or retrofit that reliably adds them. Our guide on [how old is too old for an opener](/blog/old-garage-door-opener-safety/) explains how to date yours.

Replace it. A new opener is $275 to $600 installed, and a unit that age in a Florida garage has also spent thirty summers above 100°F, so its board and gears are on borrowed time anyway. On a house with children or pets, this is not a repair decision.
