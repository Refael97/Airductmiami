## What the remote just told you

The remote and the wall button reach the opener by different routes. The remote talks by radio to a receiver inside the motor unit. The wall button is wired: two thin low voltage wires run down the wall from the motor unit, often across the ceiling first, to the console by the house door.

So if the remote opens the door, the opener has power, the drive moves the door, and the logic board and receiver work. Whatever has failed is on the wired route. That is the console, the wire, or the two terminals on the back of the motor unit where the wire lands.

It is the mirror image of [a remote that is not working](/blog/garage-door-remote-not-working/), where the wall button still opens the door.

## Read the console

Most modern consoles have a light, or a lit button, and it is powered through those same two wires. It is the quickest clue you have.

**Dark.** Nothing is reaching it from the opener. The wire is cut, shorted, or loose at a terminal. This is the most common result.

**Lit but the button does nothing.** Power is getting there, so the console itself is the suspect. Buttons wear out after thousands of presses, and the electronics in a smart console fail.

**Works on some days.** Often worse when it is humid. That is a corroded terminal or a wire half cut through.

You can rule out the lock button. On consoles that have one it disables remotes and keypads and leaves the wall button alone.

## The one that is not really a button fault

If the wall button opens the door but you have to hold it down the whole way to close it, and the remote will not close it at all, the button is fine. On many openers that is how the door behaves when the safety sensors cannot see each other: a held wall button overrides them, because you are standing there watching.

Check the two small lights on the photo eyes by the floor. One dark or flickering is your fault. Our guides on [safety sensors](/blog/garage-door-safety-sensors-explained/) and [a flashing opener light](/blog/garage-door-opener-blinking-light-codes/) take it from there. Do not make holding the button a habit. The sensors are what stops the door on a child or a pet.

## The wire is the usual suspect

That thin cable is stapled along the wall and ceiling, and in Florida garages the same things happen to it again and again.

**A staple or a screw through it.** A staple driven too hard shorts the two conductors together. It shows up most after somebody has put up shelving, a ceiling storage rack or a cabinet, and one screw has gone straight through the run.

**Pinched or chewed.** Crushed behind a shelf, rubbing against the door track, or chewed by rodents.

**Ants.** They nest in the console and in the terminal block on the motor unit, and a nest in there is enough to break the contact.

**Corrosion.** Bare copper at the terminals goes green in a humid garage, and the connection comes and goes with the weather.

Follow the wire by eye from the console to the opener and look for a staple sunk into it, new screws on its line, or a flattened section.

## The test at the opener

If you are comfortable on a ladder, one test separates the console and wire from the opener. Take the two console wires off the terminals on the back of the motor unit, then touch a short piece of wire across both terminals at once. On most openers that does exactly what a press of the wall button does.

**The door moves.** The opener is fine. The fault is in the console or the wire down to it.

**The door does not move.** The opener's wall control input has failed, which is the logic board.

Our guide on [an opener that quit working](/blog/garage-door-opener-quit-working/) uses the same check when nothing works at all.

## After a new opener or a new board

If the wall button died the day a new opener or a new logic board went in, the console almost certainly does not match. Many current openers expect their own console and ignore the simple doorbell style button the old unit used. Rewiring the old button will not change that; the matching console will.

## After a lightning storm

A surge does not only arrive through the outlet. It travels along the console wire too. Sometimes it kills just the console, and sometimes it takes the part of the board that reads the console while the radio receiver survives. That is why "the remote works but the wall button died after the storm" is such a common Florida call in summer. If the jumper test does nothing, it is the board.
