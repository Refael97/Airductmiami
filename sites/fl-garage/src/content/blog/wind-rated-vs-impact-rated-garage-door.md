## The short version

Every garage door installed in Florida has to carry a wind rating. That is the Florida Building Code, and the permit is reviewed against it. An impact rating is a second, separate test that some doors also pass. A wind rated door resists pressure. An impact rated door resists pressure and has survived a piece of lumber fired at it. When a homeowner says hurricane door, they almost always mean the second one.

## What a wind rating actually is

A wind rating is a design pressure, written as two numbers in pounds per square foot: a positive number for the wind pushing the door in, and a negative number for the suction pulling it out as the wind wraps around the house. The negative number is usually the larger one, and it is the one that pulls a door out of its track.

The pressure a door has to hold is not one figure for the whole state. It comes from the design wind speed on the map for your site, the exposure of the lot (open ground and water raise it), the height of the wall, and where the door sits on it. A door near a corner of the house is in a higher pressure zone than one in the middle of a long wall. The building department and the contractor pulling the permit work out the required pressure for your address, and the door on the permit has to meet or exceed it.

To meet a higher pressure, the manufacturer adds horizontal struts across the sections, uses heavier section skins and stiles, specifies more and larger fixings into the jamb, and tests the whole assembly at a stated size. The rating belongs to that model at that size with that hardware. Change the struts or the fixings and you no longer have the rated door.

## What an impact rating adds

An impact rated door has passed the large missile impact test. A nine pound piece of two by four lumber is fired at the door at about 50 feet per second, and the door is then cycled through thousands of rounds of positive and negative pressure to prove that the impact did not open a path for the wind. The test is meant to stand in for the roof tile, the fence board or the branch that becomes a projectile in a hurricane. The Miami-Dade protocols and the statewide standards both use it, with differences in detail.

The reason it matters is the failure the code is written around. The garage door is the largest opening in the house. If it is breached, the house pressurizes from inside and the roof is at risk. Holding pressure keeps the door in its track; resisting impact keeps a hole from opening in it.

An impact rated door is also a wind rated door. The reverse is not true.

## Which one you need where

| Where | What the door needs |
|---|---|
| Miami-Dade and Broward, the HVHZ | A Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance. The door we install here is impact rated |
| Rest of Florida | A Florida Product Approval, an FL number, with a design pressure that meets the site. Whether impact is also required depends on how the site sits under the code |
| Any address | A building permit, pulled by a licensed contractor, with the rating on it |

## How to read the label on your existing door

Open the door partway, or fully, and look at the inside face of the sections and the end stiles, the vertical edges of each section. The label is usually on the back of the top or bottom section or on an end stile, sometimes on a sticker near the track. On an older door it may be faded, painted over or gone.

A rating label carries some or all of these:

1. **Manufacturer and model.** The series name and the section design.
2. **Size.** The width and height the rating was tested at.
3. **Design pressure.** Two numbers, positive and negative, in psf.
4. **Approval number.** An FL number for a Florida Product Approval, or an NOA number, which looks like a two digit year, a four digit date and a two digit suffix, for a Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance.
5. **Impact.** The word impact, the words large missile, or a reference to the impact test standard. If none of those appear, the door is wind rated only.
6. **Date of manufacture.**

If you find a design pressure and an approval number, the door is rated, and the approval number tells you which system. If you find a label with only a model name and a date, or no label at all, treat the door as unrated. The statewide Florida Building Code took effect in 2002, and many doors installed before then outside South Florida were never rated.

## What the label does not tell you

A label proves what the door was when it left the factory. It does not prove how it was installed. The rating assumes the struts on the drawing, the fixings on the drawing, and the track and jamb the approval calls for. A rated door screwed into a rotted wood jamb, or fitted without its top strut because the installer was in a hurry, is not the door on the label. That is what the permit inspection exists to catch, and our guide to [garage door permits in Florida](/blog/garage-door-permit-florida/) explains what the inspector looks at.

It also does not tell you how the door has aged. Thirty years of salt air on a rated door in Kendall or Hollywood leaves rusted stiles, thinned bottom sections and fixings that have lost their grip. The rating does not decay on paper. It decays in the steel, and our guide to [rust and Florida humidity](/blog/garage-door-rust-florida-humidity/) shows what that looks like.

## The insurance side

Florida's wind mitigation inspection, on form OIR-B1-1802, records whether openings, including the garage door, have rated protection. A rated door may qualify for a premium credit under the state's mitigation discount rules; the schedule is your insurer's, and you should ask them for it before you buy, along with what the inspector needs to see on the garage door. Our guide to the [wind mitigation insurance credit](/blog/garage-door-wind-mitigation-insurance-credit/) covers the form. We do not promise a dollar figure.

## Bracing is not an impact rating

Retrofit bracing kits add vertical posts to an existing door so it holds more pressure. They have Florida Product Approval in most of the state and are not the accepted route in the HVHZ. A braced door is a stronger door, and it is not an impact rated door: nothing about a post behind the sections stops a projectile going through them.
