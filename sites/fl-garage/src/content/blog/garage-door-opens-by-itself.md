## Start by taking it seriously

An open garage door is an open door to your house. In Florida the garage is attached, the door into the kitchen or laundry is often unlocked, and the garage itself holds the tools, the bikes and often the golf cart.

So before you diagnose anything, secure it tonight. Unplug the opener at the ceiling outlet and pull the manual release, or use the lock button on the wall control if your unit has one. That mode disables every remote and keypad and takes one press. Our guide on [garage door locks and security](/blog/garage-door-locks-and-security/) covers the manual slide lock for a door that has to stay shut.

Then work through the causes below, in order, because they are ordered by how often they turn out to be the answer.

## One: a stuck or wet button

Unglamorous and extremely common, and in Florida the weather gets a vote.

The wall control, a remote in a car door bin, a remote clipped to the golf cart visor, a keypad outside with a swollen or jammed key. Any button held closed sends a continuous signal, and the opener does what it is told.

The outdoor keypad is the Florida suspect. It sits on the jamb taking direct sun all morning and a storm every afternoon for half the year. The rubber keys swell, the housing fills with moisture, and a key sticks down or the board inside shorts across two contacts. A keypad that is discolored, cracked or has water behind the cover is the first thing we look at.

**How to test it.** Take the batteries out of every remote in the house, the cars and the cart, and the battery out of the keypad. If the phantom opening stops, put them back one at a time over a few days until it returns. If it does not stop, you have ruled out remotes entirely and that is worth knowing.

## Two: an old opener without rolling codes

This is the cause most people have never heard of and it explains a lot of the strangest cases.

Garage door openers made before roughly 1993 used a **fixed code**, set with a row of small dip switches inside the remote and a matching row inside the opener. There were only a few hundred or a few thousand possible combinations depending on the model.

That means a neighbor can be carrying a remote set to the same combination as yours. They press it for their door and yours opens too.

Florida makes this more likely than most places. Whole subdivisions in [Coral Springs](/service-areas/coral-springs/), [Pembroke Pines](/service-areas/pembroke-pines/), [Brandon](/service-areas/brandon/) and Palm Harbor were built in a few years in the 1980s and early 1990s, with the same builder fitting the same brand of opener on every house. Where those openers are still running, they share a small pool of codes on a street of identical doors. Older single door houses in [Hialeah](/service-areas/hialeah/), Hollywood and St. Petersburg have the same units for a different reason: the opener was never replaced.

**How to tell which you have.** Open the remote. A row of tiny switches inside means a fixed code opener. A circuit board and a battery means rolling codes.

**The fix.** If your opener is fixed code, no adjustment makes it secure. You either add a rolling code receiver to the existing motor, which is possible on some units, or you replace the opener. Given that a fixed code opener is at minimum thirty years old, has spent those years in a hot garage, and predates the safety sensor requirement, replacement is the honest recommendation rather than the upsell. ## Three: radio interference

Openers work on a narrow radio band, most commonly 315 MHz or 390 MHz in the United States. Several household things transmit noise in that region.

**LED and CFL bulbs.** The most common offender by a distance. Cheap LED bulbs in the opener itself, or in fittings near it, can produce enough radio noise to cause phantom operation and to shorten remote range. Bulbs sold as rated for garage door openers are shielded for exactly this reason.

**Nearby transmitters.** Florida has several military air bases, from MacDill in Tampa to Patrick on the Space Coast and Homestead in the south, and some of them use adjacent frequencies. If you live near one and the problem is intermittent and time patterned, this can be the cause, and the fix is an opener on a different frequency.

**New electronics in the house.** Baby monitors, wireless doorbells, cheap wireless cameras. If the problem started the week something new arrived, unplug that thing and watch.

## Four: a wiring fault

The wall control connects to the motor unit with two low voltage wires running along the wall and up. If those two wires touch anywhere along their run, the opener reads it as somebody pressing the button.

Staples driven through the insulation, a wire pinched behind a shelving unit, rodent damage, or corrosion where the wire runs along a damp block wall will all do it. Corrosion is the Florida one: the wire sits against block that wicks moisture up from the slab, and the insulation and the copper both give up over the years.

**How to test.** Disconnect the two wall control wires at the motor unit. If the phantom operation stops, the fault is in that circuit and not in the radio side. This is a five minute test and it splits the problem cleanly in half.

## Five: a failing logic board

Last on the list because it is last in frequency, but Florida produces more of these than most places. A board with a failed relay, water in the housing, or damage from a lightning surge can trigger the motor with no input at all. Heat does its part too: a board that has spent ten summers above 100°F is not the board it was.

Symptoms that point this way: the door operates with every remote battery removed and the wall control disconnected, and the opener does other odd things such as lights turning on unprompted or the unit running for a moment when power comes back after an outage.

On an older unit, weigh the board price against a new opener before committing. Our guide on [an opener that has quit](/blog/garage-door-opener-quit-working/) covers that decision.

## The diagnostic order, condensed

1. Secure the garage tonight. Unplug it or use lock mode.
2. Remove every remote and keypad battery. Wait. Does it stop?
3. Open a remote. Dip switches inside means a fixed code opener and that is very likely your answer.
4. Change the opener bulb for one rated for the job.
5. Disconnect the wall control wires at the motor unit. Does it stop?
6. If it still happens with no remotes and no wall control, the board has failed.

## Two things worth doing regardless

**Lock the door between the garage and the house.** Whatever the cause turns out to be, this is the difference between losing a bicycle and losing everything.
