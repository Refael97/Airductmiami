July and August are our busiest months, and not for the reason people expect. Springs fail on cycles, not temperature. What the Florida summer does is cook the opener, rust everything that is not sealed, throw water and spiders at the safety system every afternoon, and then cut the power. This is what fails, why, and what to do about it before June.

![A garage door opener motor unit on the garage ceiling](/images/opener-motor-unit.webp)

## What fails in July and August

| Fault | Cause | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Opener dead or intermittent | Logic board or capacitor cooked by a 100°F garage, or a lightning surge | Opener repair $95 to $300 |
| Opener runs, door does not move | Stripped plastic drive gear in an older chain drive unit | Opener repair $95 to $300 |
| Door will not close, light flashes | Photo eyes blocked by webs, mud daubers, rain or low sun | Usually free to fix yourself |
| Door reverses on the way down | Friction up from rust or dry hinges, force limit marginal | Tune-up $85 to $160 |
| Loud bang, door will not open | Spring at the end of its cycle count | Spring replacement $150 to $350 per spring |
| Door heavy after a storm | Water in a wood door, or a cable that has rusted through | Cable repair $95 to $300 |
| Stuck inside during an outage | No battery backup, manual release never tested | Battery backup adds $60 to $150 |

## Heat: the opener is the victim

The door itself does not mind heat. The opener does. A closed Florida garage runs 100°F to 110°F on a summer afternoon, and the motor unit hangs at ceiling height where it is hottest.

**Logic boards and capacitors.** Electronics age faster hot. A board that was fine in March starts dropping out at three in the afternoon in July, then works again at nine at night when the garage cools. That pattern, working in the evening and failing in the heat of the day, is the board or the start capacitor. Our guide on [what to do when a garage door opener quits working](/blog/garage-door-opener-quit-working/) walks through the diagnosis. [Opener repair](/services/garage-door-opener-repair/) is $95 to $300.

**Plastic drive gears.** Older chain drive openers, the kind still hanging in 1990s garages across Coral Springs, Pembroke Pines, Brandon and Oviedo, drive the chain through a nylon gear. Heat softens it, grease runs out of it, and one day the motor hums but the door does not move, with a small pile of white shavings on top of the unit. Our guide on [an opener that runs while the door does not move](/blog/opener-runs-door-doesnt-move/) covers it. On a unit over fifteen years old, a gear kit at $95 to $300 is worth weighing against a new [opener installation](/services/garage-door-opener-installation/) at $275 to $600.

**Lubricant.** Heat thins it and runs it off the spring, the hinges and the rollers, which is why we say three rounds a year here rather than two. Our guide on [how to lubricate a garage door in Florida humidity](/blog/how-to-lubricate-a-garage-door/) covers what survives.

## Humidity: everything that is not sealed rusts

A summer garage sits at 80 to 90 percent humidity, and nothing dries out until November.

**Springs** pick up rust between the coils, which adds friction and starts the pitting that becomes a crack. **Cables** rust where they anchor at the bottom bracket, which sits in water after every storm. **Hinges** bind at the pivot. **Open bearing steel rollers** seize from the inside.

The result on the driveway is a door that has gradually got heavier and slower, until the opener's force limit reads the extra friction as an obstruction and reverses. Our guide on [why a garage door opens slowly](/blog/garage-door-opens-slowly/) covers that chain. Near either coast, salt air speeds all of it up, and our guide on [salt air corrosion](/blog/salt-air-garage-door-corrosion/) explains which parts go first.

## Afternoon storms: water and spiders

From June to October, most of the state gets a storm most afternoons, and two things follow.

**Water at the seal.** Runoff pools at the bottom of the door every day. A flattened or torn bottom seal lets it inside, where it sits against the bottom brackets and the jamb. A wood door swells and gets heavier. A wood jamb rots and the track brackets loosen. Check the seal monthly through the wet season, and if water is coming in every afternoon, our guide on [garage door jamb replacement in Florida](/blog/garage-door-jamb-replacement/) explains what it leads to.

**Photo eyes.** The sensors at the bottom of the track are the most common summer call we get, and most of them cost nothing to fix. Spiders and mud daubers build on the lenses through the summer, wind-driven rain gets across the beam, and on a west facing garage the low afternoon sun can blind the receiving eye directly. The door will not close, the opener light flashes, and the fix is wiping both lenses, checking the brackets have not been knocked, and shading the sensor from the sun. Our guides on [why a garage door will not close](/blog/garage-door-wont-close/) and [what the flashing opener light means](/blog/garage-door-opener-blinking-light-codes/) go through it.

## Lightning: the surge that kills the receiver

Florida has more lightning strikes than any other state, and summer is when they happen. A surge through the power line, or through the low voltage wiring to the wall control or the sensors, takes out the opener's logic board. The pattern is simple: the door worked before the storm, and afterward the remotes do nothing, or nothing does anything at all.

A surge protector on the opener outlet is cheap and worth fitting in May. If the board is already gone, [opener repair](/services/garage-door-opener-repair/) is $95 to $300, and on an old unit a new opener at $275 to $600 is the better spend.

## Power outages: the manual release and the battery

After a storm, the power goes out, and a garage door with no battery backup is a wall. This is where Florida differs from everywhere else: the outage is not a rare event, it is a season.

**Test the manual release in May**, not during the outage. With the door closed, pull the red cord and lift the door by hand. If it lifts easily and stays where you put it, the springs are balanced and you can get out. If it is heavy or drops, the springs are weak, and you need to know that now rather than with a hurricane coming. Our guide on [how to use the manual release](/blog/garage-door-manual-release-how-to/) covers reconnecting afterward.

**Battery backup** adds $60 to $150 to any new opener and runs the door for a couple of days of normal use with the power out. On a [smart opener](/services/smart-garage-door-opener-installation/) at $350 to $750 it is usually built in, along with a phone app that tells you whether the door is closed when you are evacuated three counties away. Our guide on [garage door battery backup for power outages](/blog/garage-door-battery-backup-power-outage/) covers what to look for.

## Springs: more cycles, not more heat

Springs fail on count. Summer in Florida means school is out, the family is home, and the garage door runs ten or twelve times a day instead of six. A spring that had a few hundred cycles left in June is gone by August. [Spring replacement](/services/garage-door-spring-replacement/) is $150 to $350 per spring, both together adds $120 to $250, and a same-day call-out with the car inside adds an emergency premium of $150 to $300. Our guide on [how long garage door springs last in Florida](/blog/how-long-do-garage-door-springs-last/) explains how to estimate your own count.

## The May checklist

Everything below takes half an hour and prevents most of the above, and it doubles as the pre-hurricane check.

- **Balance test.** Release the opener, lift the door halfway, let go. If it does not hold, the springs are already tired.
- **Lubricate properly.** Garage door silicone or lithium spray on hinges, roller bearings, spring and end bearings. Not the track.
- **Clean the photo eyes** and check they point at each other.
- **Check the bottom seal.** Replace it if it is cracked, flattened or torn.
- **Tighten the hardware.** Hinges, track brackets, opener mount.
- **Test the manual release.**
- **Surge protector** on the opener outlet.
- **Confirm the door is rated.** If it is not, our guide on [preparing a garage door for a hurricane](/blog/prepare-garage-door-for-hurricane/) is the next thing to read.
