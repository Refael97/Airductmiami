## Get your car out first

If the door is shut and you need to be somewhere, pull the red release cord hanging from the trolley on the rail. That disconnects the door from the opener, and the door will now lift by hand.

**One warning.** A properly balanced door lifts easily and stays where you put it. If the door is very heavy or slams back down, the spring has failed and the opener was only masking it. Do not stand under it and do not try to prop it. That situation is a spring call, not an opener call, and it is the one genuinely dangerous scenario on this page.

## Work out which kind of dead it is

There are three, and they point at completely different faults.

**Nothing at all.** No light, no sound, no response from any control. Power problem.

**Light works, motor does nothing.** Power is fine. The problem is in the controls, the logic, or the safety circuit.

**Motor hums or runs but the door does not move.** Power and logic are fine. Something mechanical has failed between the motor and the door.

Identify yours before reading further.

## Nothing at all: start at the outlet

**Is the opener plugged in?** Genuinely. Vibration walks plugs out of ceiling outlets over years, and somebody may have unplugged it to use the socket.

**Is the outlet live?** Plug a lamp or a phone charger into it.

**Has the GFCI tripped?** Garage outlets are usually GFCI protected, and the reset button may be on that outlet, on another outlet in the garage, or in the panel. Damp weather trips them. This is the single most common cause of an opener that died overnight for no reason.

**Has a breaker gone?** Check the panel and look for one sitting between on and off.

**Is the bulb the only thing dead?** Some units share a circuit between the bulb and the logic in a way that makes a blown bulb look like a bigger failure. Change it before diagnosing further.

If the outlet is live and the opener is genuinely getting power and doing nothing at all, the transformer or the board has failed.

## Light works, motor does nothing

Power is fine, so the opener is choosing not to run.

**Is it in lock or vacation mode?** Many wall controls have a lock button that disables remotes, and some disable everything. It is easy to press by accident. Look for a lit indicator on the wall control.

**Are the safety sensors happy?** The sensors only block downward travel, so if the door will go up but not down, that is sensors. Our guide on [what a flashing opener light means](/blog/garage-door-opener-blinking-light-codes/) covers this in full.

**Does the wall button work when remotes do not?** Then the problem is the radio receiver or the remotes, not the motor.

**Do neither work?** Disconnect the two wall control wires at the motor unit and briefly touch them together. If the opener runs, the wall control or its wiring is the fault. If nothing happens, the fault is inside the unit.

## Motor hums but nothing moves

This is the most common way an opener dies, and it is usually the cheapest to fix.

**A stripped drive gear.** On chain and belt drive units built by Chamberlain, which includes a great many LiftMaster and Craftsman models, a plastic worm drive gear sits between the motor and the drive shaft. It is a deliberate wear part and it strips after years of use, faster if the door has been heavy. The symptom is exactly this: a motor that runs, sometimes with a grinding note, and a trolley that does not move. A gear and sprocket kit is an inexpensive part and this repair is well worth doing on almost any unit.

**A failed capacitor.** The capacitor gives the motor the kick it needs to start. When it fails, the motor hums, gets hot and does not turn. Also inexpensive, and also worth doing.

**A broken trolley or carriage.** The trolley is what the arm attaches to. If it has cracked, the chain or belt moves and the door does not. Look along the rail while someone presses the button.

**A snapped chain or belt.** Visible from the floor. You will see it hanging.

**The door is disconnected.** Somebody pulled the release cord and never reconnected it. Pull the cord again with the door closed and run the opener, and the trolley should re-latch. This happens more often than anyone admits.

## The thing people get wrong

A very large share of the openers we are called out to have not failed at all. **The door failed and took the opener down with it.**

An opener is not designed to lift a garage door. The springs lift the door. The opener is there to give the balanced door a nudge and to hold it in place. When a spring fatigues, the door gets heavier, and the opener starts doing work it was never built for. It strips gears, burns capacitors, overheats and trips its own overload protection.

Replace that opener without touching the spring and you will be replacing the new one too.

**The thirty second test.** Close the door. Pull the release cord. Lift the door by hand to waist height and let go.

- Stays put: the door is balanced, and your opener genuinely is the problem.
- Drops or rockets up: the spring is wrong, and the opener is a symptom.

Do this before you spend money on any opener. It costs nothing and it is the single most useful thing on this page.

## Repair or replace

**Worth repairing at almost any age:** gear and sprocket kits, capacitors, limit switches, safety sensors, remotes, wall controls, chains and belts, trolleys.

**Usually not worth repairing on an older unit:** the logic board. Boards for discontinued models get scarce and the price climbs. When a board on a unit past fifteen years costs more than half of a new opener, the honest answer is a new opener, and we will say so rather than sell you the board.

**Replace regardless if the unit predates 1993.** Openers from before then have no photo eye safety sensors, which have been required by federal law on residential units since 1993, and many use fixed codes that a neighbour's remote can trigger. Age is not the argument there. Safety is.
