## Get your car out first

If the door is shut and you need to be somewhere, pull the red release cord hanging from the trolley on the rail. That disconnects the door from the opener, and the door will now lift by hand.

**One warning.** A balanced door lifts easily and stays where you put it. If the door is very heavy or slams back down, the spring has failed and the opener was only masking it. Do not stand under it and do not try to prop it alone. That is a spring call, not an opener call, and it is the one dangerous scenario on this page. Our guide on [a car trapped in the garage](/blog/car-trapped-in-garage/) covers that situation step by step.

## Work out which kind of dead it is

There are three, and they point at completely different faults.

**Nothing at all.** No light, no sound, no response from any control. Power problem.

**Light works, motor does nothing.** Power is fine. The problem is in the controls, the logic, or the safety circuit.

**Motor hums or runs but the door does not move.** Power and logic are fine. Something mechanical has failed between the motor and the door.

Identify yours before reading further.

## Nothing at all: start at the outlet

In Florida this is the most common way an opener dies, and it is usually not the opener.

**Is the power out?** After an afternoon storm it may be out on your street and not the next one. Check a light in the house.

**Has the GFCI tripped?** Garage outlets are GFCI protected, and the reset button may be on that outlet, on another outlet in the garage, or near the panel. Storms, humidity and the surge when power comes back all trip them. An opener that died overnight during a storm and has no light at all is this until proven otherwise.

**Is the opener plugged in?** Vibration walks plugs out of ceiling outlets over years.

**Has a breaker gone?** Check the panel and look for one sitting between on and off.

**Did lightning hit nearby?** Florida records more lightning strikes per square mile than any other state, and the corridor between Tampa and Orlando is the busiest part of it. A strike does not have to hit the house to put a surge through the opener. If the opener was fine before a storm and is completely dead after it, with the outlet live, the transformer or the logic board has taken the surge.

If the outlet is live and the opener is getting power and doing nothing, the transformer or the board has failed.

## Light works, motor does nothing

Power is fine, so the opener is choosing not to run.

**Is it in lock or vacation mode?** Many wall controls have a lock button that disables remotes, and some disable everything. Look for a lit indicator on the wall control.

**Are the safety sensors happy?** The sensors only block downward travel, so if the door will go up but not down, that is sensors. Our guide on [what a flashing opener light means](/blog/garage-door-opener-blinking-light-codes/) covers this in full.

**Does the wall button work when remotes do not?** Then the problem is the radio receiver or the remotes, not the motor. Our guide on [a remote that stopped working](/blog/garage-door-remote-not-working/) is the next stop.

**Do neither work?** Disconnect the two wall control wires at the motor unit and briefly touch them together. If the opener runs, the wall control or its wiring is the fault. If nothing happens, the fault is inside the unit.

## Motor hums but nothing moves

This is the most common mechanical way an opener dies, and it is usually the cheapest to fix.

**A stripped drive gear.** On chain and belt drive units built by Chamberlain, which includes a great many LiftMaster and Craftsman models, a plastic worm drive gear sits between the motor and the drive shaft. It is a wear part, and a garage that spends every summer above 100°F softens that plastic and shortens its life. The symptom is exactly this: a motor that runs, sometimes with a grinding note, and a trolley that does not move. Look for white plastic shavings on top of the motor housing. A gear and sprocket kit is an inexpensive part and worth doing on almost any unit.

**A failed capacitor.** The capacitor gives the motor the kick it needs to start. Heat is what kills capacitors, and Florida garages supply plenty of it. When it fails the motor hums, gets hot and does not turn. Also inexpensive, and also worth doing.

**A broken trolley or carriage.** The trolley is what the arm attaches to. If it has cracked, the chain or belt moves and the door does not.

**A snapped chain or belt.** Visible from the floor.

**The door is disconnected.** Somebody pulled the release cord during the last outage and never reconnected it. Pull the cord again with the door closed and run the opener, and the trolley should re-latch. This happens after every storm season.

## The thing people get wrong

A very large share of the openers we are called out to have not failed at all. **The door failed and took the opener down with it.**

An opener is not designed to lift a garage door. The springs lift the door. The opener gives the balanced door a nudge and holds it in place. When a spring fatigues, the door gets heavier, and the opener starts doing work it was never built for. It strips gears, burns capacitors and overheats.

**The thirty second test.** Close the door. Pull the release cord. Lift the door by hand to waist height and let go. Stays put: the door is balanced, and your opener is the problem. Drops or shoots up: the spring is wrong, and the opener is a symptom. Do this before you spend money on any opener.

## Repair or replace

**Worth repairing at almost any age:** gear and sprocket kits, capacitors, limit switches, safety sensors, remotes, wall controls, chains, belts and trolleys.

**Usually not worth repairing on an older unit:** the logic board. Boards for discontinued models get scarce and the price climbs. When a board on a unit past fifteen years costs more than half of a new opener, the honest answer is a new opener, and we will say so rather than sell you the board.

**Replace regardless if the unit predates 1993.** Openers from before then have no photo eye safety sensors, which federal law has required on residential units since 1993. Our guide on [old opener safety](/blog/old-garage-door-opener-safety/) explains the rest of what is missing.

Two Florida additions when you do replace. Put a surge protector on the opener outlet, because the next storm is coming. And choose a unit with battery backup, which adds $60 to $150 and means the door still runs when the power is out, which after a storm is exactly when you need it. Our guide on [battery backup and power outages](/blog/garage-door-battery-backup-power-outage/) covers how long a backup lasts.
