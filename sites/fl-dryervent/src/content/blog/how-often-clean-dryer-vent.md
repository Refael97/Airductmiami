Most Florida households should have the full dryer vent run cleaned once a year. If your dryer runs every day, you have shedding pets, or the duct travels a long way through the attic to a roof cap, plan on every six months. A short, straight run through an exterior wall used by one or two people can often go longer, as long as the outside flap still opens fully and loads dry in a single cycle.

## Why one schedule does not fit every home

Lint builds up at a rate set by two things: how much laundry goes through the dryer, and how easily the duct carries that lint outside. A large family drying towels every day produces far more lint than a retired couple. A duct that runs straight out the wall clears itself better than one that climbs two stories and turns four times on the way.

That is why a fixed calendar date is only a starting point. The table below is the schedule we suggest after seeing the run, and it is the one we adjust once we have airflow readings from your home.

| Household and vent | Suggested cleaning interval |
|---|---|
| One or two people, short wall vent | Every 12 to 18 months |
| Typical family, wall vent | Every 12 months |
| Typical family, attic run to roof cap | Every 6 to 12 months |
| Large family or daily drying | Every 6 months |
| Shedding dogs or cats in the home | Every 6 months |
| Condo with a long horizontal run | Every 6 to 12 months |
| Condo on a shared riser | On the building's schedule, plus your own branch |
| Rental or vacation home with heavy turnover | Every 6 months |

## Household size and laundry volume

The single best predictor is how many loads you dry. Count a typical week. A household drying five or six loads a week sits comfortably on a yearly schedule. Once you are past ten or twelve loads, every six months makes more sense.

Florida adds its own twist. Beach towels, pool towels and bedding are dried often here, and thick cotton sheds more lint than most clothing. Sand that rides home in towels also ends up in the screen housing and the transition hose.

## Run length and where the vent ends

The length and shape of the duct matter as much as laundry volume. Every bend slows the air, and slow air drops lint. Long horizontal stretches in an attic collect it along the bottom of the duct, and any low point holds moisture that turns lint into a damp paste.

Roof terminations deserve extra attention. The air has to rise before it leaves, and when the dryer stops, lint that did not make it out can settle back down. Roof caps also sit where you cannot see them, so a stuck damper or a nest goes unnoticed for months. If yours ends on the roof, err toward the shorter interval in the table.

## Pets

Pet hair behaves differently from cotton lint. It is light, it clings, and it knits together with lint into a felt that grabs onto joints and fittings. Homes with dogs or cats that shed heavily see faster buildup even when the laundry volume is modest. Dog beds and blankets in the dryer speed things up further.

If you have pets, shaking out pet bedding outdoors before it goes in the washer helps, and so does a second look at the lint screen in the middle of a long cycle.

## Condo versus house

A house usually has a duct that serves one dryer and runs to the nearest exterior wall or up to the roof. A condo vent often has to cross the unit to reach an outside wall, running horizontally above a ceiling for a long distance. Some older buildings connect each unit to a shared vertical riser.

Long horizontal runs need cleaning more often than short ones. Shared risers raise the stakes because lint from many units collects in one place. In those buildings your own cleaning only covers your branch, and the riser itself depends on the association. ## Signs you should not wait for the calendar

A schedule is a minimum, not a promise. Book a cleaning sooner if you notice any of these:

- Towels or jeans need a second cycle to dry.
- The top of the dryer or the clothes feel unusually hot.
- The laundry room gets warm and sticky while the dryer runs.
- The outside flap barely moves when the dryer is on.
- Lint or nesting material shows at the outside hood.
- You smell something scorched.

A scorched smell means stop using the dryer until the vent is checked. Our article on the [signs of a clogged dryer vent](/blog/signs-of-a-clogged-dryer-vent/) explains each warning in more detail.

## What you can do between cleanings

The habits that stretch the time between visits are simple:

- Clean the lint screen after every load, not every few loads.
- Wash the screen with warm soapy water every few months if you use dryer sheets, since they leave a film that blocks air.
- Keep the transition hose behind the dryer short and free of kinks.
- Check from outside that the flap opens when the dryer runs.
- Keep plants and mulch clear of a wall hood.

These steps slow buildup, but they do not reach the duct itself. Our page on [DIY dryer vent cleaning](/blog/diy-dryer-vent-cleaning/) covers what a homeowner can safely handle and where a brush kit runs out of reach.

## Setting your schedule

The simplest way to land on the right interval is to start with the table, then let the first professional cleaning tell you more. During a [dryer vent cleaning](/services/dryer-vent-cleaning/) we measure airflow before and after and see how much lint came out. A light layer after a year means your schedule is fine. A packed duct means it should be shorter.

Write the date on a piece of tape inside the laundry room cabinet or set a phone reminder. Most people mean to keep up with it and simply lose track of when it was last done.
