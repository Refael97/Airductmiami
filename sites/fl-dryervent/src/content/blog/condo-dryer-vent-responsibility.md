Who is responsible for a condo dryer vent is decided by your building's declaration of condominium and its rules, not by a single statewide answer. In many Florida buildings, the unit owner maintains the dryer, the transition hose and any duct that serves only their unit, while the association maintains shared risers, roof fans and terminations that serve more than one unit. Read your documents, then confirm it with the manager in writing before anyone books work.

## Why the answer varies by building

Florida condominiums are governed by their own declarations, which define where a unit ends and the common elements begin. Some declarations say that anything serving only one unit belongs to that unit, wherever it runs. Others treat everything inside the walls and ceilings as common. Some spell out dryer vents by name and many do not.

That is why two neighbors in different buildings can get different answers to the same question. Start with the declaration and any maintenance responsibility chart the association has adopted.

## Who usually handles what

This table shows the most common split we see. Your documents override it.

| Component | Usually maintained by | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Dryer and lint screen | Unit owner | Your appliance, your responsibility |
| Transition hose behind the dryer | Unit owner | Listed, under 8 feet, not in the wall |
| Duct serving only your unit | Often unit owner | Whether the declaration calls it a limited common element |
| Exterior wall hood serving only your unit | Varies | Whether exterior surfaces are association property |
| Shared vertical riser | Association | Cleaning schedule and who to call |
| Roof exhaust fan on a shared riser | Association | Whether it runs and when it was serviced |
| Roof cap serving several units | Association | Who has roof access and how it is booked |

## How condo vents are usually built

Condo dryer vents take one of a few shapes, and each one changes the cleaning.

**Individual runs to an exterior wall.** Each unit has its own duct, often crossing the unit above a dropped ceiling to reach a balcony or exterior wall. These runs tend to be long and horizontal, which lets lint settle. They need cleaning more often than a short house vent, and the hood may be several floors up.

**Individual runs to the roof.** Common on top floors and in some low rise buildings. Each unit has its own cap on the roof, and roof access goes through management.

**Shared risers.** Each unit connects a short branch into a vertical duct that serves the units stacked above and below. The riser ends at the roof, sometimes with an exhaust fan. Lint from every connected dryer collects in the same riser.

## The shared riser problem

Shared risers change the stakes. When one unit skips cleaning, its lint ends up in a duct that everyone above and below depends on. When the riser itself clogs, every unit on it dries slowly, and moist air can push back into units whose dampers do not seal.

Cleaning only your branch helps your dryer, but it does not clear the riser. A riser has to be cleaned as a building system, from the roof and from the connections, in a coordinated visit. That is the association's job in most buildings, and it is the main reason building wide cleaning is scheduled rather than left to each owner.

## What to ask the board or manager

Send these questions in writing. The answers tell you what you can book yourself and what has to wait for the building.

1. Does the declaration or a maintenance chart say who maintains dryer vents?
2. Does my unit have its own run to the outside, or does it connect to a shared riser?
3. If there is a riser, when was it last cleaned, and is there a schedule?
4. Is there a roof exhaust fan on the riser, and is it working?
5. Who handles the exterior hood or roof cap for my unit?
6. What are the rules for contractors: insurance certificates, elevator booking, work hours?
7. Can owners join a building wide cleaning day and pay for their own unit?

Many associations will ask any contractor for a certificate of insurance before work starts. We are insured and can provide one to management.

## What you can do in your own unit

Whatever the building's answer, some things are always yours. Clean the lint screen after every load. Keep the transition hose short, straight and connected. Pull the dryer out once in a while and look for lint on the floor or a crushed hose. If your loads take longer to dry, tell management, because in a riser building it may not be your duct at all.

Condo vents with long horizontal runs usually need cleaning more often than house vents. Our guide on [how often to clean a dryer vent](/blog/how-often-clean-dryer-vent/) includes condo intervals.
