## The arithmetic

Start with what a visit costs the company that sends it.

A service visit means a van, fuel, commercial insurance, a stocked parts
inventory, workers' compensation, and a technician's time including travel.
In the New York metro area an hour of a skilled trade technician's time alone
runs $50 to $150 before any of the rest of it.

So the question answers itself. **A $29 call-out does not cover the drive.**
It does not cover the fuel on the Southern State on a Tuesday afternoon. It
does not cover the fifteen minutes of paperwork afterwards.

That is not a criticism of the number. It is a description of it. Nobody sets
a price below cost by accident, and no company in this trade is running a
charity for Long Island homeowners. **The number is not the product. Getting
into your driveway is the product**, and the $29 is what it costs the company
to buy that.

## What the fee is actually buying

For the company, three things.

**Access.** A technician standing in your garage is in a completely different
commercial position from a voice on the phone. You have already waited in for
the appointment. Your car may be trapped. The comparison shopping you would
have done at a keyboard is now much harder to do with somebody watching.

**Commitment.** You have made a decision, however small. Behavioural research
on commitment and consistency is not controversial, and the trade knows it
without needing to read the literature.

**Disassembly.** This is the important one. Once the door is apart, the choice
is no longer "this company or another company". It is "say yes to this
company or spend the evening with a garage door in pieces". Those are not the
same decision and the second one is much easier to win.

## The law has a name for this pattern

The Federal Trade Commission publishes [Guides Against Bait
Advertising](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-238)
at 16 CFR Part 238, issued under Section 5 of the FTC Act. The guides define
bait advertising as an offer that is not made in order to be fulfilled, but
in order to obtain leads and switch the customer to something else, usually
more expensive.

Two points in those guides matter for the situation you are in.

The first is that the test is intent, not outcome. An advertisement is bait
if the advertiser never meant the advertised offer to be the transaction.

The second is stronger and less well known. **Correcting the impression later
does not cure it.** Under the guides, a violation can occur where the first
contact was obtained by deception, even if the true facts are disclosed to
the buyer afterwards. In other words, "but we told him the real price once we
got there" is not the defence people assume it is.

We are a garage door company, not a regulator, and whether any specific
advertisement crosses that line is not ours to decide. But the pattern the
guides describe is recognisably the pattern in this trade, and it is worth
knowing that a name for it exists in federal regulation.

## Where the money actually gets made

Once inside, the margin comes from parts, and specifically from three
properties of garage door parts that make them ideal for the purpose.

**You cannot price them from where you are standing.** You know roughly what
a dishwasher costs. You have no idea what a torsion spring costs, what wire
gauge yours is, or whether the bearing plate genuinely needed replacing.

**They are genuinely technical.** "Your springs are undersized for this door"
might be completely true. It is also unfalsifiable while you are standing
there in your socks.

**They fail in clusters, honestly.** A door that has broken a spring often
does have worn rollers and a fraying cable, because they all aged together.
This is the part that makes the whole thing work: **the upsell is frequently
real.** That is precisely why it is effective, and why "they recommended extra
parts" is not on its own evidence of anything.

If a quote for two standard springs on a standard single door lands at
$1,200, that is not a different opinion about your door. That is a different
business model.

## The five minute defence

All of it happens before anyone is dispatched, which is the only point at
which you have any leverage.

**Ask for the price of the finished repair, not the call-out.** "If it turns
out to be a broken spring, what will I pay in total?" A company that quotes
published prices can answer in one sentence. A company that cannot will
produce a reason why the question is impossible. It is not impossible. We
answer it every day.

**Ask whether the fee is credited against the work.** A legitimate diagnostic
fee comes off the bill. A bait fee does not.

**Ask what they charge for both springs.** Springs come in a manageable range
of sizes, and any competent company can give you a range on the phone.

**Get it in writing before dispatch.** A text message is writing. An email is
writing. "We will sort it out when we get there" is not.

**Check the licence.** In Nassau County, [Consumer
Affairs](https://www.nassaucountyny.gov/1547/Consumer-Affairs) on
516-571-2600 will confirm whether a home improvement licence is on file and
whether there is a complaint history. In Suffolk, the [Office of Consumer
Affairs](https://suffolkcountyny.gov/Departments/Consumer-Affairs) on
631-853-4600 does the same. Operating a home improvement business without a
county licence is illegal in both. Most people never make this call. It takes
two minutes and it filters out a great deal.

## What the consumer agencies say about it

This is not a local grievance. The Better Business Bureau publishes a
standing [scam alert on garage door
repair](https://www.bbb.org/article/scams/29292-bbb-scam-alert-garage-door-repair-scams)
built on complaints to its Scam Tracker, and it describes the same sequence:
an advertised low service fee, an inflated assessment of what the door needs,
and pressure applied at the moment the customer is least able to compare
prices.

The BBB's warning signs are worth knowing because several of them are
checkable before anyone arrives. Generic company names that are not
registered with the state. No genuine physical premises, or an address that
does not correspond to anything. A phone number and an email as the only
contact route. High pressure framing that the repair must happen now.

Its core advice is the same as ours and is worth more than any of the rest:
**get more than one quote**, because the second number is what makes the
first one legible.

Regional BBB offices reissue the warning regularly. Wisconsin's ran one in
[March 2026](https://www.wbay.com/2026/03/03/bbb-warns-homeowners-garage-door-repair-scams/).
The reason it keeps being reissued is that it keeps working.

Individual cases reach the point of absurdity. Moneywise reported a [Florida
homeowner billed roughly $12,000 for garage door work valued near
$1,500](https://moneywise.com/news/news/home-repair-scam-garage-door-overcharge-florida).
That is not a pricing disagreement. That is what happens when nobody asked
for a number before the van arrived.

## Why we publish prices instead

We charge no call-out fee at all, low or otherwise. Emergency and after-hours
dispatch carries a premium of $200 to $350, that premium is stated before we
dispatch, and it applies to the dispatch only. **The repair itself is quoted
at the same published price it would carry on any other day of the year.**

Publishing the numbers costs us the negotiating advantage that arriving with
an unknown price would give us. That is the point. If you can check our
number against a competitor's from your kitchen table at eleven at night,
without letting anyone into your garage first, then the price is doing the
work rather than the pressure.

You get a written estimate before anything is touched, and that estimate is
what you pay.

## If it has already happened

**Work not started.** Pay the agreed fee, decline, and get a second quote.
You owe nothing further.

**Work already done and the price was inflated.** File with the county
Consumer Affairs office that licensed them, Nassau on 516-571-2600 or Suffolk
on 631-853-4600. If you paid by card, ask your issuer about a dispute, and be
specific about what was agreed and when the number changed. Report it to the
[BBB Scam Tracker](https://www.bbb.org/scamtracker) so the next person
searching the company name finds it.

**Keep everything.** The advertisement or listing you called from. Any text
or email. The invoice. Photographs of the parts removed, if you still have
them. Complaints with documentation get somewhere. Complaints without it
usually do not.
