## What goes first

In order of how often we find it:

**Roller bearings.** Exposed steel bearings pick up salt, corrode, and seize. A seized roller drags in the track and eventually takes the door off it.

**Hinges.** Rust at the pivot binds the hinge, which puts stress on the section it is fixed to and eventually cracks the hinge or the steel around it.

**Springs.** Corrosion at the coils creates stress concentrations. Springs near the water fail earlier than their cycle count alone would predict.

**Bottom brackets.** The worst one. It sits at floor level in exactly the spot where water, salt and grit collect, and it anchors the cable that carries the full spring load.

**Cables.** Corrode where they anchor at the bottom bracket and where they wrap the drum.

**The panel itself.** Steel corrodes wherever the factory finish is scratched through, and it spreads under the coating from there.

## The specification that helps

**Sealed-bearing nylon rollers.** The single most useful upgrade. The bearing is protected and the wheel cannot rust. A full set is $150 to $250 installed and it roughly doubles the interval between hardware problems.

**Galvanised or stainless fixings.** Bolts, brackets and hinges. A small addition to any job that involves replacing them anyway.

**Galvanised or powder-coated springs.** More corrosion resistant than plain oil-tempered ones, at modest extra cost.

**An intact factory finish.** On a steel door, the coating is the protection. Touch up scratches promptly with matching paint rather than leaving bare steel exposed.

**A bottom weather seal in good condition.** It keeps water off the bottom section and away from the brackets.

## The routine

Twice a year rather than once, if you are on the water.

- Rinse the bottom two feet of the door and the bottom brackets with fresh water, especially after a storm surge or a nor'easter
- Lubricate springs, hinges and roller bearings with silicone or lithium spray
- Look at the bottom brackets specifically. Surface rust is normal; flaking, pitting or rust at the bolt holes is not
- Check the cables where they anchor and where they wrap the drums
- Touch up any scratches in the door finish

## What it saves

A door in a coastal location that is never serviced typically needs its first significant repair years earlier than an inland one, and the failures cluster: rollers, then a cable, then a derailment.

The routine above costs a can of lubricant and half an hour twice a year, plus a $95 to $180 annual tune-up if you would rather someone else did it. Against a $150 to $700 off-track repair, that is not a difficult trade.
