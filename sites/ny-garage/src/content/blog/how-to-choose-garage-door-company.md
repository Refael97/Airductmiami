## The signals that actually mean something

**They will quote in writing before dispatch.** The strongest single signal in this trade. It costs an honest company nothing.

**Prices are published or freely given.** A company that answers "how much" with a real range on the phone is a company that expects to charge that.

**Licensed and insured, with details on request.** Not the word on the van, the number and the certificate.

**A real arrival window.** Three hours, not "sometime Tuesday", and a call when it changes.

**A stated warranty.** Specific periods for parts and for labor, on the invoice.

**They will show you the failed part.** This one separates more companies than any technical question.

## The signals that mean less than they appear

**Age of the company.** "Since 1983" tells you a business survived, which is worth something, but it does not tell you how it prices. Some of the longest-established firms in this market still refuse to quote before arriving.

**Volume of advertising.** In this trade the heaviest advertisers are frequently the thin lead-generation operations, not the best technicians.

**A very low advertised price.** Almost always the cost of the visit rather than the work.

**Star ratings alone.** Useful in aggregate, easily gamed in isolation. Read the one and two star reviews rather than the average, and look for whether complaints are about price changing after arrival.

## The two minute check

Before anyone comes out:

1. Ask what the finished repair costs, and get the answer by text or email.
2. Ask what happens if they find something else once the door is apart.
3. Ask for the warranty on parts and on labor.
4. Ask for the license number.

Four questions. A company that handles all four cleanly will almost certainly handle the job cleanly.
