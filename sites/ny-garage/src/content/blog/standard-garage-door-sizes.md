## The two sizes that cover most houses

**Single car: 9 feet wide by 7 feet high.** This is the default across most of the United States and the one you should assume if someone says "standard".

**Double car: 16 feet wide by 7 feet high.**

Everything else is a variation on those.

## The full standard range

**Single doors**

| Size | Where you see it |
|---|---|
| 8 x 7 | Older homes and narrow lots. Tight for a modern SUV |
| 9 x 7 | The national default |
| 10 x 7 | Wider single, common on newer builds and for trucks |
| 8 x 8 | Older stock with taller openings |
| 9 x 8 | Newer builds, taller vehicles |
| 10 x 8 | Trucks, vans, roof boxes |

**Double doors**

| Size | Where you see it |
|---|---|
| 15 x 7 | Less common, older builds |
| 16 x 7 | The double car default |
| 16 x 8 | Newer builds, taller clearance |
| 18 x 7 | Wide double |
| 18 x 8 | Wide and tall, common on newer suburban construction |

**Heights** are effectively 7 or 8 feet. Seven is the historic default. Eight has become standard on newer construction because vehicles got taller, and it is what you need if you want a roof box, a van, or a car lift.

## How to measure, properly

Four numbers, not one. Get all four before you call anyone.

**1. Width of the opening.** Measure the finished opening at its widest point, wall to wall. Not the old door.

**2. Height of the opening.** Floor to the underside of the header, at the highest point of the floor. Garage floors slope for drainage, so measure at more than one point and use the shortest.

**3. Headroom.** From the top of the opening to the ceiling, or to the lowest obstruction such as a pipe, a joist or a light. Standard torsion track wants about 12 inches. Extension springs want about 10. Under that you are into low headroom track, which exists and works, and costs more.

**4. Side room.** From each side of the opening to the nearest wall or obstruction. You need roughly 3.75 inches each side for standard track, and a little more for some hardware. Openings hard up against a side wall need special brackets.

Also worth noting: **backroom**, the depth from the opening into the garage. You need the door height plus about 18 inches for the door to sit fully open, more with an opener.

Write all of those down. Any competent company will ask for them, and one that quotes a door without asking is guessing.

## Why so many New York garages are not standard

A great deal of the housing stock in Brooklyn, Queens and the older parts of Nassau and Westchester predates the standardisation of garage door sizes, and quite a lot of it predates the two car garage entirely.

What we run into constantly:

**Openings under 8 feet wide.** Garages built for cars that were much narrower than anything sold today.

**Heights under 7 feet.** Sometimes considerably under. A 6 foot 6 opening is not unusual in a converted or under-house garage.

**Almost no headroom.** Under-house garages in Brooklyn frequently have a joist or a beam directly above the opening. This is what low headroom track and jackshaft wall mount openers exist for.

**Openings that are not square.** A century of settlement means the opening can be an inch or more out of true from one side to the other, and a stock door fitted into it will bind or leave a gap.

None of this is a problem. All of it is a reason to measure rather than order from a size chart.

## What non standard costs

A custom width or height door is made to order. Expect roughly **15 to 30 percent above** the equivalent stock size, and a lead time of several weeks rather than something pulled off a shelf.

That premium is worth understanding correctly. It is not a penalty for having an unusual house. It is the difference between a mass produced item and one cut to your opening, and the alternative, forcing a stock door into an opening it does not fit, produces a door that binds, leaks and wears out early.

## Does size change the repair price?

Yes, and this is worth knowing before you get quoted.

A double door is a bigger job than a single across the board. It is heavier, so it runs on different springs. It has more rollers, more hinges, more track. Nearly every price on this site is stated as a starting price **for a standard single door**, and a 16 by 7 double will sit above that starting point for the same repair.

An 8 foot high door also carries longer track and, on a torsion setup, a longer shaft, which is why an 8 foot door costs more than a 7 foot door of the same width.

## A quick word on insulation and size

`9x7 insulated` is a search we see often, and it is worth separating two things.

Size is fixed by your opening. Insulation is a choice about the door's construction, and it is available in every standard size. An insulated door is heavier, which matters because the spring has to be matched to the door's actual weight. Fitting an insulated door on springs sized for the old uninsulated one is a genuine and common installation error, and it destroys springs early.

If you are weighing that decision, our guide on [insulated versus uninsulated doors](/blog/insulated-vs-uninsulated-garage-door/) covers when the extra cost returns something in New York and when it does not.
