## The pattern

It is consistent enough to describe as a script.

An advertisement offers a garage door repair for $49, or a service call for $29. You call. A technician arrives, takes the door apart, and comes back with a list: springs, cables, drums, bearings, rollers, and often the opener. The total is several hundred to a couple of thousand dollars, and the pressure to agree is applied while your car is trapped and your door is in pieces.

The advertised number was never the price. It was the cost of getting somebody into your driveway.

## The numbers that expose it

This works because most people have no reference point. Here are the ones that matter in the New York market.

| Job | Honest range |
|---|---|
| One torsion spring, installed | $170 to $400 |
| Two springs, installed | $320 to $700 |
| Cable repair | $100 to $350 |
| Cable tension adjustment only | $95 to $150 |
| Opener repair | $100 to $350 |
| New opener installed | $281 to $630 |
| Off-track correction | $150 to $700 |

People have reported being quoted $1,500 to replace two standard springs. That is roughly double the top of the honest range for that job.

## The five questions that stop it

Ask these before anyone is dispatched, and get the answers in writing, by text or email:

1. **What is the full price of the finished repair, not the visit?**
2. **Does that include parts, labor and disposal?**
3. **What happens if you find something else once the door is apart? Do I get a new quote before you proceed?**
4. **Is there a call-out or trip fee, and is it separate?**
5. **What is the warranty on parts and labor?**

A company that answers all five clearly has just told you a lot. A company that will not answer until a technician sees the door has also told you a lot.

## The tactics to watch for during the visit

**Everything failed at once.** On a twenty year old door, several parts genuinely can be at end of life together. But every one of them should be shown to you. A recommendation you cannot see is a recommendation you cannot check.

**Parts you cannot photograph.** Ask to keep or photograph the failed part. Reluctance is informative.

**Safety pressure.** "This door could fall on your child" used to justify an immediate decision. Real safety issues exist on garage doors and they can be explained calmly with the part in hand.

**The discount that expires today.** A price that is only valid while the technician is standing there is a sales tactic, not a price.

**Refusal to write it down.** Every legitimate quote can be written down before work starts.

## What an honest visit looks like

You get a range before dispatch. You get a written estimate before anything is touched. If something unexpected is found, work stops and you get a new number before it continues. The failed parts get shown to you. The door gets balance tested with the opener disconnected at the end. And the warranty is on the invoice rather than in a sentence.

None of that is a high bar. It is simply what the work should look like.
