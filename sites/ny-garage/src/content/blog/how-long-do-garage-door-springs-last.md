## Cycles, not years

This is the thing most people get wrong. A spring does not fail because it is old, it fails because it has been wound and unwound a certain number of times.

One cycle is one full opening plus one full closing. A standard residential torsion spring is rated for about 10,000 of them.

## Work out your own number

| Openings per day | Cycles per year | Expected life |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 730 | About 13 years |
| 4 | 1,460 | About 7 years |
| 6 | 2,190 | About 4.5 years |
| 8 | 2,920 | About 3.5 years |
| 10 | 3,650 | About 3 years |

A household with two drivers who each leave and return once a day is running four cycles. Add a school run, a dog walk and a trip to the shops and you are at eight without noticing.

This is why a spring on a Marine Park or Bay Ridge under-house garage, used constantly, fails years sooner than one on a suburban Long Island garage used twice a day.

## What shortens the life

**Cold.** Not the temperature itself, but its effect on steel already fatigued. Winter is when tired springs die.

**Salt air.** Corrosion at the coils creates stress points. On the coast, springs fail earlier than the cycle count alone would predict.

**An unbalanced door.** If the spring is undersized for the door, it is working outside its design range every single cycle.

**Poor lubrication.** A dry spring has more friction between coils and heats slightly under load. Two spray applications a year is enough.

**Rust.** Powder-coated and galvanised springs last longer near water than plain oil-tempered ones, and cost slightly more.

## High-cycle springs

A high-cycle spring is physically larger: thicker wire, more coils, longer. It stores the same energy across more material, so each coil works less hard.

| Rating | Life at 4 cycles/day | Extra cost per spring |
|---|---|---|
| 10,000 | About 7 years | Baseline |
| 20,000 | About 14 years | $80 to $150 |
| 50,000 | About 34 years | $200 to $350 |

They need more headroom than a standard spring, so they do not fit every garage. On a heavily used door they are usually the cheaper option over ten years, because the visits cost more than the springs.

## The test that tells you where you are

Pull the manual release with the door closed. Lift the door to waist height by hand and let go.

A healthy door stays there. A door that sinks slowly has springs losing tension. A door that drops has springs that are effectively finished.

Do this once a year. It takes ten seconds and it is the single most useful thing a homeowner can check on a garage door.

## Replacing before it breaks

Springs give very little warning, which is the argument for replacing on schedule rather than on failure.

The economics are straightforward. Replacing proactively costs the same as replacing reactively, $170 to $400 per spring, except you pick the day, you are not paying an emergency premium of $200 to $350, and your car is not trapped.
