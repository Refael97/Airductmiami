## Start by taking it seriously

An open garage door is an open door to your house. Most garages connect to the interior, plenty of people leave the connecting door unlocked, and a garage full of tools and bikes is worth stealing on its own.

So before you diagnose anything, secure it tonight. Unplug the opener at the ceiling outlet and pull the manual release, or use the vacation mode or lock button on the wall control if your unit has one. That mode disables every remote and takes one press.

Then work through the causes in the order below, because they are ordered by how often they turn out to be the answer.

## One: a stuck button

Unglamorous and extremely common.

The wall control, a remote in a coat pocket, a remote in a car door bin, a keypad outside with a swollen or jammed key. Any button held closed sends a continuous signal, and the opener does what it is told.

A remote at the bottom of a bag with something pressing on it will do this all day. So will a keypad that has taken years of rain and has a key stuck down. So will a wall control with a cracked case.

**How to test it.** Take the batteries out of every remote in the house. Genuinely every one, including the one in the car you barely drive and the one in the kitchen drawer. If the phantom opening stops, put them back one at a time over a few days until it returns. If it does not stop, you have ruled out remotes entirely and that is worth knowing.

## Two: an old opener without rolling codes

This is the cause most people have never heard of and it explains a lot of the strangest cases.

Garage door openers made before roughly 1993 used a **fixed code**. You set it with a row of small dip switches inside the remote and a matching row inside the opener. There were only a few hundred or a few thousand possible combinations depending on the model.

That means a neighbour, a delivery driver, or someone visiting a house down the street can be carrying a remote that happens to be set to the same combination as yours. They press it for their own door and yours opens too. They will never know they did it.

It also means the code can be captured. Equipment that records a fixed code transmission and plays it back is cheap and legal to own. Rolling code openers, which is everything sold since the mid nineties, generate a new code on every single press, and a recorded code is useless the moment it has been used once.

**How to tell which you have.** Open the remote. If there is a row of tiny switches inside, you have a fixed code opener. If there is only a circuit board and a battery, you have rolling codes.

**The fix.** If your opener is fixed code, no adjustment makes it secure. The technology does not support it. You either add a rolling code receiver to the existing motor, which is possible on some units, or you replace the opener. Given that a fixed code opener is at minimum thirty years old and is also missing three decades of safety improvements, replacement is usually the honest recommendation rather than the upsell.

## Three: radio interference

Openers work on a narrow radio band, most commonly 315 MHz or 390 MHz in the United States. Several household things transmit noise in that region.

**LED and CFL bulbs.** The most common offender by a distance. Cheap LED bulbs in the opener itself, or in fittings near it, can produce enough radio noise to cause phantom operation and to shorten remote range dramatically. Bulbs sold as rated for garage door openers are shielded for exactly this reason.

**Nearby transmitters.** Some military installations, airports and emergency services use adjacent frequencies. If you live near a base and the problem is intermittent and time patterned, this can genuinely be the cause, and there is very little to be done except move to an opener on a different frequency.

**New electronics in the house.** Baby monitors, some wireless doorbells, cheap wireless cameras. If the problem started the week something new arrived, unplug that thing and watch.

## Four: a wiring fault

The wall control connects to the motor unit with two low voltage wires running along the wall and up. If those two wires touch anywhere along their run, the opener reads it as somebody pressing the button.

Staples driven through the insulation during a garage tidy-up, a wire pinched behind a new shelving unit, rodent damage, or corrosion where the wire has sat against a damp wall will all do it.

**How to test.** Disconnect the two wall control wires at the motor unit. If the phantom operation stops, the fault is in that circuit and not in the radio side. This is a five minute test and it splits the problem cleanly in half.

## Five: a failing logic board

Last on the list because it is last in frequency, but it happens. A board with a failed relay or water damage can trigger the motor with no input at all.

Symptoms that point this way: the door operates with every remote battery removed and the wall control disconnected, and the opener does other odd things such as lights turning on unprompted or the unit running for a moment on power up.

On an older unit, weigh the board price against a new opener before committing. Boards for discontinued models are scarce and expensive, and a thirty year old opener with a failed board is usually telling you something.

## The diagnostic order, condensed

1. Secure the garage tonight. Unplug it or use vacation mode.
2. Remove every remote battery in the house. Wait. Does it stop?
3. Open a remote. Dip switches inside means a fixed code opener and that is very likely your answer.
4. Change the opener bulb for one rated for the job.
5. Disconnect the wall control wires at the motor unit. Does it stop?
6. If it still happens with no remotes and no wall control, the board has failed.

Steps one to five cost nothing and can be done in an evening. They resolve the great majority of cases.

## Two things worth doing regardless

**Lock the door between the garage and the house.** Whatever the cause turns out to be, this is the difference between losing a bicycle and losing everything.

**If your opener is pre 1993, plan to replace it.** Not because of this fault alone, but because a fixed code opener also predates modern safety sensor requirements and modern auto reverse behaviour. Openers from that era are old enough that the security problem is the least of what is wrong with them.
