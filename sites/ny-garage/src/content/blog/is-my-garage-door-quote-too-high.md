## The three questions that settle it

**1. What exactly am I paying for?**

An itemised answer. Parts, labor, call-out, disposal. A single number with no breakdown is not a quote, it is a figure.

**2. What specification are the parts?**

For springs: wire size, inside diameter, length and cycle rating. A 20,000 cycle spring genuinely costs more than a 10,000 cycle one and it should be a deliberate choice you were told about.

**3. What is the door?**

Single or double, steel or wood, insulated or not, standard or custom. Every one of these legitimately moves the price, and a quote at the top of the range for a standard single door needs a reason.

## Legitimate reasons a quote is high

**Double or oversized door.** Twice the weight, two springs, heavier hardware.

**Wood or custom door.** Heavier, needs a heavier spring, parts may be model specific.

**High-cycle springs.** Rated for 20,000 or more cycles rather than 10,000. Worth it on a door used many times a day, and it should be presented as an option rather than assumed.

**More than one thing failed.** Genuinely common on an old door. Every part quoted should be shown to you.

**Difficult access.** Shared alley, steep driveway, or a garage that needs the truck parked a long way off.

**Genuine emergency dispatch.** $200 to $350, stated separately, and never applied to the repair price itself.

## Illegitimate reasons

Being in a hurry. Having a trapped car. Being quoted after the door was already taken apart. A discount that expires when the technician leaves. Parts that were replaced without being shown to you.

## What to do with a quote you think is too high

Say you want to think about it. That is a complete sentence and you do not owe an explanation.

If work has not started, you owe nothing beyond an agreed call-out fee. If a technician becomes difficult at that point, that is worth noting and worth reporting.

Then get a second quote, and give the second company the same description you gave the first, so you are comparing the same job.
