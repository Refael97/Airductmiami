## What the jamb actually does

The jamb is the wooden frame lining your garage opening: a leg up each side and a header across the top. It looks like trim. It is not trim.

Three things depend on it.

**The track brackets screw into it.** The vertical track on each side of your door is fixed to the jamb, and everything the door does mechanically runs through those fixings. Soft wood means brackets that work loose, and loose brackets mean a door that drifts out of alignment and eventually comes off its track.

**The weatherstrip nails to it.** The rubber or vinyl strip that seals the sides and top of your door is fastened to the jamb. It cannot seal against wood that has gone soft or lost its straight edge.

**The door closes against it.** The jamb is the stop. It is the difference between a garage that stays dry and one that does not.

## Why New York eats jambs from the bottom

Every jamb we replace has rotted from the ground up, and the reason is the same every time.

The bottom six to twelve inches of the jamb sits at the exact point where the driveway meets the garage floor. That is where snow gets pushed by the plough, where road salt gets carried in on tyres and then melts, where meltwater pools before it drains, and where the sun never quite reaches to dry things out.

Salt is the real villain. It holds moisture against the wood long after plain water would have evaporated, and it does that for the whole winter, every winter.

**Compounding factors we see constantly:**

- A driveway resurfaced higher than it used to be, so the jamb bottom now sits below grade
- A gutter or downspout discharging near the opening
- A garage floor that has settled and now slopes inward
- Untreated pine jambs on houses from the sixties and seventies, which were never going to survive this
- A failed bottom seal on the door, letting water in that then sits against the jamb from the inside

## How to check yours

Two minutes, no tools beyond a screwdriver.

**The screwdriver test.** Push the tip into the wood at the bottom of each leg, hard enough that you would expect it to resist. Sound wood resists and the tip barely marks it. Rotten wood accepts the screwdriver like polystyrene and what comes away is damp and stringy.

Test at floor level, then at six inches, then at a foot. That tells you how far up the rot has travelled.

**Look for the secondary signs.**

- Paint bubbling, flaking or lifting in the lower two feet
- Track brackets that move if you push them, or screws standing proud
- Daylight visible around the sides of the closed door
- Weatherstrip that has come loose or that no longer touches the door
- A dark, damp tide line up the wood
- Insect activity, since carpenter ants find wet wood before you do

**Check both sides and the header.** People check the side they park next to and miss the other. The header rots less often, but it happens where a roof or a gutter above it has failed.

## Repair or replace the leg

**Repair the bottom** if the rot is confined to roughly the lowest foot and everything above the screwdriver test comes back solid. The bad section is cut out and a new piece spliced in.

Be aware of the limitation: rot runs further up the grain than it shows at the surface. A splice that looks clean can leave damp wood sitting behind the new piece, where it carries on quietly. A good repair cuts back further than the visible damage.

**Replace the whole leg** if more than about a third of it is soft, if the rot has reached the height of a track bracket, or if the wood has moved enough that the leg is no longer straight. A jamb that is out of true means a door that never seals properly no matter what weatherstrip goes on it.

## Do the jamb before the door, not after

This is the part worth paying attention to if you are planning a new door.

A new garage door is fitted to the opening you have. Its track brackets screw into your jamb. Its weatherstrip seals against your jamb. If that jamb is rotten:

- The brackets go into soft wood and work loose within a season
- The door drifts out of alignment, and misalignment is the most common cause of a door coming off its track
- The new weatherstrip cannot seal against a soft, uneven edge
- Water keeps getting in, and now it is getting in around a door you just paid for

We would rather quote you for the jamb and the door together and have you understand why, than fit a good door onto bad wood and be back within a year. If a company quotes you a new door on a visibly rotten jamb without mentioning it, that tells you something about the company.

## What it costs

Jamb work is quoted on the job because the variables are real: how many legs, how much of each, what wood, whether the header is involved, and whether the track has to come off and go back on.

As an honest range for the New York metro, a bottom repair to one leg sits in the low hundreds and a full replacement of both legs and the header sits in the high hundreds. Done alongside a new door installation it costs less than as a separate visit, because the track is coming off anyway.

New garage door installation starts at $1,056 and typically runs $1,056 to $4,620.

## How to stop it happening again

**Use treated lumber or a composite.** Pressure treated pine is the standard answer and it is what we fit. PVC and composite jambs cost more, will not rot at all, and on a house where this has already happened twice they are worth the difference.

**Keep the bottom seal working.** A failed door seal lets water in that then sits against the jamb from the inside, which is how jambs rot on the face you never look at. The seal is a cheap part.

**Fix the drainage.** If a downspout discharges near the opening, move it. If the driveway slopes toward the garage, that is a bigger job but it is the actual cause.

**Clear snow away from the opening, not against it.** A ploughed or shovelled pile against the jamb is salt water held against wood for a month.

**Look at it once a year.** The screwdriver test in autumn, alongside the rest of a [maintenance visit](/services/garage-door-tune-up/), catches this while it is still a repair rather than a replacement.
