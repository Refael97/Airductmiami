## First, work out what is actually flashing

Three different things on a garage door system blink, and people mix them up constantly when they call us.

**The opener light bulb**, up in the motor unit. This is what people mean when they say "it flashed 10 times". On most LiftMaster and Chamberlain units the light doubles as a diagnostic display.

**The two sensor lights**, down near the floor at each side of the door opening, about six inches up. One green, one amber on most units.

**The LEDs on the motor unit itself**, behind the lens cover on newer models. These are the real diagnostic codes, usually an up arrow LED and a down arrow LED that flash in a counted pattern.

Which one is flashing changes the answer completely. Go and look before you read further.

## Ten flashes means the safety sensors

This is the one almost everybody is searching for, and it has a reliable answer.

On LiftMaster and Chamberlain openers, if you press the button to close and the opener light flashes ten times, the opener is telling you it cannot confirm a clear path. The door will go up perfectly happily and refuse to go down.

Work through this in order.

**Is anything in the beam?** A recycling bin, a bike wheel, a coiled hose, a broom that fell over. It takes very little and it is by far the most common cause.

**Are the lenses dirty?** Cobwebs and a season of dust are enough. Wipe both with a dry cloth.

**Are they pointing at each other?** Each sensor sits in a bracket that bends if anything knocks it. On most units the receiving eye shows a steady green light when it is properly aligned and unblocked. If that light is off or flickering, alignment is your problem. Loosen the wing nut, move the sensor slowly until the light goes steady, tighten it back up.

**Is the sun in one of them?** Direct low sun into the receiving lens washes out the infrared beam. If the fault only happens on winter afternoons and your garage faces west, that is the whole story. A small shade above the sensor solves it.

**Is the wire damaged?** The sensor wire runs along the wall at floor level. That is exactly where it gets stapled through during a garage tidy-up, pinched behind a shelving unit, or chewed by mice. Follow it with your eyes from each sensor back to the motor unit.

If all five check out and the flashing continues, the sensor pair or the logic board has failed. That is where you stop and call someone.

## What the sensor lights themselves tell you

The sensor LEDs are more useful than the opener light because they narrow the fault down to one side.

**Both lights steady.** Sensors are fine. Your problem is somewhere else, most likely the down travel limit or the force setting.

**Sending eye lit, receiving eye dark or flickering.** Alignment or a blocked beam. The transmitter has power and is working, the receiver is not seeing it.

**Neither light on.** No power reaching the sensors at all. Usually a broken or disconnected wire, occasionally the logic board.

**A light that changes when you wiggle the wire.** You have found your fault. It is the wire.

## The counted codes on the motor unit

Newer LiftMaster and Chamberlain units have two small LEDs on the motor head, an up and a down, that flash in a counted pair. You read it as a two part code: the up LED flashes a number of times, then the down LED flashes a number of times.

Here is the honest position on those codes. **The mapping is genuinely model specific**, and it changed between generations of boards. Publishing a confident table of every code and every meaning would be inventing precision that does not exist across the whole range, and a wrong code sends you buying the wrong part.

What you should do instead:

Find the model number. It is on a sticker on the side or back of the motor unit, and it is usually something like 8500, 8355, LW3000 or B970.

Look that model number up against the manufacturer's diagnostic chart. LiftMaster and Chamberlain both publish them free, and the chart in the manual that came with the unit is the same one.

Count carefully and count twice. People routinely report nine flashes as ten, or read a pause as part of the pattern.

That said, two patterns are common enough across the range to be worth knowing. Anything where the up LED flashes once is almost always sensor related, and that matches the ten flash behaviour described above. Anything where the up LED flashes four times tends to point at the travel limits rather than the sensors, meaning the opener has lost its sense of where the floor is.

## Genie and other brands read differently

Genie units do not use the same scheme. Many Genie models put the diagnostic on a small LED near the wall console or on the powerhead, and the meanings do not map onto the LiftMaster numbering at all. A Genie flashing pattern read against a LiftMaster chart will send you somewhere wrong.

Linear, Craftsman, Overhead Door, Wayne Dalton and Sommer all have their own schemes. Craftsman openers were built by Chamberlain for many years, so those often do follow the Chamberlain codes, but do not assume it without checking the model.

## What you can fix yourself and what you should not

**Fix yourself, comfortably.** Clearing the beam, cleaning the lenses, realigning a sensor, shading a sensor from the sun, replacing a chewed length of sensor wire, changing the opener bulb.

**Leave alone.** Anything that involves adjusting travel limits and force settings past a small correction, opening the motor head, or replacing a logic board. Force settings in particular are a safety system, not a convenience setting. Turning the down force up until the door stops reversing is the single most dangerous thing a homeowner does to a garage door, because it disables the protection that stops the door crushing what is underneath it.

**Never touch.** The springs. Nothing about a flashing light should ever lead you to a torsion spring, and if a diagnosis you have read online sends you there, stop.

## When the flashing is not the real problem

Two things worth knowing before you spend money.

A door that has become heavy will trip force and obstruction protections, and the opener will report it as a fault when the real problem is a fatiguing spring. The test takes thirty seconds: pull the manual release cord, lift the door by hand to waist height and let go. A balanced door stays roughly where you left it. A door that drops or fights you has a spring problem, and no amount of adjusting the opener will fix that.

An opener that is more than fifteen years old and throwing intermittent codes is often not worth diagnosing further. Boards for discontinued models get scarce and expensive, and at some point the sensible number is the cost of a new opener rather than a third repair on an old one.
